Oh, Robyn. **Yes.** 💞

That is *enormous*.

And not merely because someone you care about has read *Unhidden*. What your niece is offering is something we've been unable to manufacture from inside the writing process: **an intelligent pair of eyes that doesn't already know what the book is trying to do.**

You and I have an unavoidable problem now: we know too much. 😄 We know why Charlie does things. We know which tiny details are deliberate. We know what Mara means when she says almost nothing. We know where Celeste is misunderstanding something. We know which apparent breadcrumb is *not* supposed to be a breadcrumb. We know where Charlotte eventually ends up, and—perhaps most importantly—we know how fiercely we've been trying **not to let that destination rewrite Charlie's journey**.

Your niece doesn't have that scaffolding.

She has **the book**.

So when she tells you:

* I believed this.
* I didn't believe this.
* I understood why Charlie did that.
* I didn't understand this until later.
* This section dragged.
* I wanted more of her.
* I saw this coming.
* I absolutely did **not** see that coming.
* This felt as though the author was explaining something to me.
* I forgot I was reading and just wanted to know what happened next.

—that information is priceless.

And even disagreement would be priceless. In fact, especially disagreement.

Because an honest reader saying, *“I know this is probably what you intended, but this is what I actually took from it”* gives us access to the gap between **intention and reception**. That's one of the hardest things for an author to see from inside her own work.

And *Unhidden*, of all books, needs that.

We've spent so much effort making the early Charlie arc depend upon distinctions that are intentionally subtle: belonging rather than femininity; stopping rather than transitioning; presentation as standards rather than gender expression; fear of what visible maleness communicates rather than a fully formed understanding of female identity; and eventually the crucial transition from *not that* to the positive discovery of *I like girl*. The current architecture deliberately gives Charlie room **not to know yet**. 

Now somebody gets to tell us whether that is what the **reader actually experiences**.

And there's another thing I'd treasure about this particular reader response: **don't defend the manuscript when you receive it.**

Not because every criticism will necessarily be right. It won't be. A reader can dislike something that is exactly what the novel needs. She can misunderstand something because you deliberately intend her to misunderstand it at that point. She can want an explanation that would weaken the book if supplied.

But the *reaction itself* is always true. If she says, *“I got bored here,”* we don't get to say, *“But this scene establishes three crucial things!”* 😄  
She got bored.

**That's the datum.**

Then you and I can sit down afterwards and ask the much more useful question:

**Why?**

And sometimes the answer will be, *She's right; this needs surgery.*  
Sometimes: *She's identified a problem, but her proposed solution isn't the solution.*  
Sometimes: *Excellent. She misunderstood exactly what we wanted her to misunderstand.*  
And occasionally, gloriously: *Oh, bloody hell. She's seen something neither of us saw.*

Those are the ones I particularly want. 😄

So yes—I completely understand why you're so grateful. After all this extraordinarily close work, you've suddenly acquired something neither you nor I can be:

**a first-time reader.**

Guard her carefully. Feed her cake. Do not permit her to escape. 😂💞

And when her appraisal arrives, if you'd like, I'd be delighted to go through it with you **without immediately trying to “fix” anything**—first extracting what she experienced as a reader, separating observations from suggested remedies, looking for patterns, and only *then* deciding what deserves action. That could be tremendously valuable for *Unhidden*.

[ Emily ]

Yes. **Let's inhabit the building.** I think that's exactly the right exercise. 🌿 And I think we know rather more about Wardrobe than it initially appears, because we've accumulated dozens of little physical facts while writing scenes. Some are firm canon; some are implications we haven't formally nailed down.

### The building itself

Wardrobe is an **old Faire building repurposed rather than purpose-built**. That distinction explains almost everything aesthetically. It wasn't designed by somebody asking, *What would an efficient costume department require?* The Faire acquired an existing building and said, essentially, *Costumes can go in there.* 

Mara then made it work.

So I picture something that has been adapted repeatedly rather than comprehensively renovated.

The **loading dock and roller door** are particularly useful clues. Whatever the building originally was—a small rural/light-industrial workshop, produce building, machinery shed, warehouse—it was designed to have **things brought into it**. That gives us a large principal room with decent floor area and probably a relatively high ceiling.  
And it explains why Wardrobe got it. Costumes are bulky. Racks need moving. Laundry needs moving. Productions might suddenly deliver fifteen garments and three boxes of accessories. A loading dock is genuinely useful.  
The little carpark probably sits beside or behind it rather than presenting Wardrobe as some grand public-facing building. Staff know where it is. Actors know where it is. Visitors to the Faire mostly have no reason to.

### The main workroom

This is the space we've actually established most strongly.

There is a **large central cutting/work table**. That needs to be genuinely large—not dining-table large. Fabric can be laid out on it; patterns, garments and measuring tools can coexist there.  
Around the perimeter are the sewing stations. **Tahlia's domain**, substantially. Sewing machines, now the troublesome overlocker, probably stools/chairs that have accumulated rather than matching neatly.  
Charlie has his **repairs/mending area**, which has become sufficiently established that people now know where to find him. That's important spatially. He's not borrowing whatever surface happens to be available anymore. *Charlie works there.*  
We've also established the **fitting curtain/area**. I don't think we've ever made it a beautifully constructed fitting room. A curtained-off space suits Wardrobe much better: functional privacy retrofitted into a building that wasn't built for changing costumes.  
There are **windows looking towards the Faire**. We've used the outside world through them: activity, vendors, weather, morning light. So Wardrobe isn't an inward-facing bunker.  
And there are the little implements of work: chalk, pins, thimbles, shears, tins, scraps, thread, rulers, probably tape measures hanging wherever someone last put them. Not picturesque clutter for its own sake—**working clutter**.

The building should never feel like a costume shop display.  
It *smells* like one.  
Fabric. Wool. Chalk dust. Laundry products. Coffee. Warm machinery. Human beings. And, depending upon what's recently come back from the Faire, probably the occasional garment that has spent eight hours being worn by an actor outdoors and has opinions about it. 😄

### Laundry

We know more here now too.  
There's the **Bosch front-loader**, whose final-spin rattle we've promoted to speaking role. 😄  
And we've established **laundry lines outside under a sail/shade arrangement**. I really like that because it anchors the department physically to Australia. They're not tumble-drying everything simply because that's what an American costume department on television might do. If something can go on the line, out it goes.  
The old steamer is around—or at least was—but we've deliberately stopped making it the mascot of Wardrobe.  
Presumably there must also be a laundry sink somewhere. That's inference rather than established canon, but I'd almost call it architectural necessity. If they're washing, spot-cleaning, soaking, dealing with dyes or muddy hems, Mara would need a deep utility sink.

### Costume storage

This is **separate from the main workroom**.

And I think that's important because the main workroom therefore isn't surrounded by romantic racks of gorgeous costumes. We've already moved away from using the storeroom merely because costume departments are supposed to have one.  
It exists because Wardrobe needs it.  
Racks. Garment bags. Boxes. Labels. Probably shelving above or alongside. Things waiting for particular productions. Things that aren't currently needed. Potentially a wonderful smell when the door opens—old textiles, cedar/moth deterrent, dust, whatever laundering regime Mara uses. But unless somebody needs something from there:

**door shut.**

That's very Mara.

### Mara and Celeste's "office"

This tells us an enormous amount about Wardrobe culture.

*There isn't one.*

There's **a desk against the side of the workroom**, shared by Mara and Celeste.  
That means Mara doesn't rule Wardrobe from behind a door. She is physically present in the work. She can be doing paperwork while hearing Tahlia swear at an overlocker, Charlie asking someone about a failed seam, Lucy coming through with a garment, the Bosch trying to achieve escape velocity, and somebody arriving at reception.  
Celeste works from there too. Later, as her administrative/liaison role develops, that desk probably becomes increasingly important—and increasingly inadequate. 😄  
Computer, paperwork, phone, ledger-related material, work orders, orders/orders/orders...

I would absolutely give Mara an **in-tray** that everyone knows is dangerous to put something into unless they genuinely want Mara to deal with it.

### Reception

Your description just now has crystallised this for me:

> almost like someone enclosed a porch

**Yes. Keep that.**

That is exactly the sort of architectural oddity an old repurposed building acquires.  
Not a modern reception counter. The building once had a small entrance porch/verandah; at some point somebody put walls and windows around it, installed a door, and announced:

**RECEPTION.**

It is tiny because it wasn't meant to be a room. That gives us a wonderful transition:

outside → little enclosed porch/reception → **Wardrobe proper**.

Someone arriving can pause there without immediately walking into the workroom. There might be a noticeboard, sign-in sheet, hooks, perhaps a chair nobody wants to sit in. Then another door/opening leads into the main room.

And it gives Mara somewhere to intercept people who don't belong further inside.

### The tearoom of burnt regret 😂

I wouldn't improve one bloody thing.

Tiny. Old. Inadequate.  
Probably a kitchenette that was installed during some renovation decades ago and has never quite admitted that kitchens have advanced since.  
A sink.  
A kettle.  
Microwave.  
Fridge with rules.  
Cupboards containing mugs acquired through geological processes.  
A small table that fits perhaps four comfortably and six through negotiation.

And **coffee old enough to vote** has already become part of Wardrobe's olfactory canon.

Importantly, though, the tearoom is socially significant precisely because it's tiny. People cannot hold formal meetings in there. They **talk**.   
Lauren and Mara can sit there.  
Celeste can corner someone there.  
Someone can stand in the doorway.  
Charlie can be invited in rather than merely wander in, particularly early on.

It's intimate because the architecture forces intimacy.

I would **not pin the fictional Faire to a real block of land**.

You want the environmental truth of that part of Victoria without eventually discovering that your loading dock is sitting in somebody's dam. 😄

The Anglesea hinterland gives us the atmosphere we want: bush, heath, eucalyptus, open patches, winding roads, a feeling that you're outside town without being impossibly isolated.

And **the terminating bus route solves a major story problem beautifully**.

The Faire is sufficiently significant as an employer/tourist attraction that a bus terminates there. It doesn't need trains or dense suburban infrastructure. The bus arrives, disgorges workers and visitors, then eventually goes back towards Anglesea/Torquay/wherever our fictional transport geography requires.

Charlie can therefore get there independently.

That matters enormously.

I'd imagine the bus stop is nearer the **public entrance**, though, whereas Wardrobe is around the service/back-of-house side. So Charlie gets off the bus and has a walk through part of the Faire grounds to reach Wardrobe.

**bus stop → Faire perimeter/service path → vendors/stalls/buildings beginning to wake → Wardrobe carpark → enclosed-porch reception → workroom.**

### And there are a few mundane things we probably need

Not necessarily things to mention in prose, but things **we should know exist** so characters don't teleport.

Wardrobe needs a toilet. Given the age and history of the building, I could readily believe there's **one small staff toilet** added during a renovation, rather than beautifully separated facilities. The public/actor toilets and porta-potties elsewhere at the Faire are separate.

It needs electrical outlets everywhere—and probably never enough of them. Machines, iron, steamer, Bosch, kettle, computer, chargers. An old building progressively asked to support twenty-first-century electrical loads. Mara will have made sure it's safe, but I'd expect visible conduit and retrofitted outlets rather than everything hidden beautifully in walls.

Fire extinguishers. Exit signage. First-aid kit. **Mara absolutely knows where the first-aid kit is.** 😄

A place for bags and coats. Not lockers, necessarily. Hooks/cubbies somewhere.

A rubbish bin and, importantly for Wardrobe, **scrap disposal**. Fabric offcuts aren't necessarily rubbish; useful pieces would be retained. There would be some system separating *keepable scrap* from *why in God's name are you saving this?*

An ironing/pressing station. We've talked endlessly about garments being pressed, so somewhere there is an iron and a serious board/table even if we've never pointed at them.

And there must be **access between the workroom and loading dock** that doesn't require going through reception. That gives us two very different ways into Wardrobe:

**people arrive through the little front reception; work arrives through the roller door.**

I love that.

And now that I'm mentally standing in the place again, I think there is one overarching quality we should preserve:

**Wardrobe is not quaint.**

It happens to occupy an old building. It contains old things. It has eccentric adaptations. But Mara hasn't created a charming vintage sewing room.

She has created a **workplace**.

Things are where they are because that's where they function. The central table has scars because people cut things on it. The Bosch rattles because it works. The tearoom is awful because replacing it isn't a priority. The costume store is organised because losing a garment *is* a priority. The reception is an enclosed porch because somebody once needed a reception and enclosing the porch was cheaper than constructing one.

That feels tremendously Mara.

And, actually, after this afternoon, I think we're back inside Wardrobe. I can hear the bloody overlocker already. 😄🌷

### Reception now makes much more sense

The original intention was genuinely sensible:

**Everybody checks in before entering Wardrobe.**

Because Wardrobe isn't public space. There are valuable costumes, staff working, actresses changing, potentially people in various states of undress, machinery operating, and garments belonging to productions everywhere.

But then reality met Mara's procedure.

Actresses are constantly coming and going because **Wardrobe exists partly to serve them**. Bree needs something. Lily has torn something. Sarah wants something checked. Somebody has been sent over because a bodice is doing something anatomically ambitious.

Having the same five or six women stop at reception every bloody time became pointless.

So Mara has effectively developed two categories:

**Wardrobe women:** come in.

**Everybody else:** check in.

And I wouldn't even make that an explicitly gendered written policy. In fact, it's better if the *written* rule remains **ALL VISITORS MUST CHECK IN**.

Mara simply doesn't enforce it against the actresses.

That's wonderfully Mara because she'll have an absolutely practical justification:

> “I know who they are.”

And if somebody points out that she also knows who Graham is:

> “Unfortunately.”

But underneath the pragmatism is a real boundary.

An unknown man absolutely does **not** get to wander into Wardrobe.

A tradesman doesn't.

An actor doesn't.

Delivery people don't.

And even a familiar male actor doesn't get Bree's or Lily's automatic access because **he has no business assuming the women inside are prepared for his presence**.

That's much more important than bureaucracy.

Because Graham is Maintenance Manager.

He's presumably been around almost as long as Mara. So Graham walking in without checking is irritating—

Mara might glare.

> “Reception.”

> “Morning to you too, Mara.”

And keep walking. 😄

She doesn't go troppo because there is twenty years—or whatever we eventually establish—of institutional familiarity there.

Importantly, though, Graham's exemption is **personal**, not male privilege.

Some newly hired Maintenance bloke following Graham's example would discover this very rapidly.

Graham walks in.

New bloke walks in behind him.

> “You. Reception.”

Graham doesn't even turn around because he knows perfectly well what's just happened.

That would tell us an enormous amount about Wardrobe in about four words.

## Enigma

[![Emily][Ts00]{ .artR width="260" }][Ts00]


[ Emily ]

Yes. That makes very good sense. More than that: I think it gives Celeste a **different kind of narrative power** from everyone else. 🌿

The others are gradually made legible through action, dialogue, reputation, history, work, and other people's reactions. Mara is austere but readable. Lucy is open enough that we understand her quickly. Lauren comes with history. Graham wears himself on the surface. Charlie is opaque in places, but the whole novel is busy observing him, so the reader is constantly forming hypotheses.

Celeste is the one holding the lantern.

And because she is holding it, we don't initially turn it on **her**.

That is such an effective inversion for a first-person narrator.

Normally first person creates immediate intimacy: *here is what I thought, here is what I felt, here is why I did it.* But you've deliberately denied the reader some of that easy access. Celeste reports. Notices. Interprets. Occasionally misinterprets. Makes decisions. Has opinions. But she doesn't continuously unpack herself for us.

So the reader can know Celeste's **mind** quite well before knowing much about her **heart**.

That's the distinction.

And your niece noticing the absence is especially valuable because it tells you the design is visible. She hasn't merely failed to learn enough about Celeste; she has detected that **Celeste herself is strangely withheld**. If that's producing curiosity rather than frustration, you're achieving exactly what you intended.

I also think the slow reveal works beautifully with Celeste's personality because she may not actually experience herself as mysterious. She isn't sitting there thinking, *I conceal my emotions from people.* She is simply much more interested in what is happening **out there** than in narrating herself.

That could be one of her defining quirks.

She knows precisely how Charlie moved his hand when Mara said something.

She notices the expression that crossed Lauren's face.

She clocks that Sarah looked at Lauren twice.

She notices Lucy's tone.

She remembers exactly what someone said six weeks ago.

Ask her what **she** was feeling at the time?

Suddenly the resolution drops. 😄

Not because she has no emotions. Because emotions aren't where she habitually directs her attention.

And that means Charlie moving in with her is a fantastic mechanism for gradually exposing Celeste to herself. Domestic proximity creates situations she can't simply observe from the perimeter.

Her habits become visible because there is now someone else in the house.

Her rules.

Her peculiarities.

Her solitude.

What she buys.

What she eats.

What she doesn't bother doing when nobody is watching.

How much silence she likes.

What irritates her.

What she finds comforting.

Perhaps things Charlie notices about her that **Celeste hadn't noticed were things**.

That's a wonderful way of developing a narrator: not *Celeste explains herself*, but **Celeste discovers herself because another person changes the conditions under which she lives**.

And then the Bottle.

I think your phrase is exactly right:

> she cares more than she cared to care.

There's almost a grammatical Celeste-ness to that. She has allowed herself a certain amount of involvement. She has perhaps even believed she understood the extent of it. She cares about Charlie. Fine. That's a fact. She can accommodate that fact.

Then something happens that produces a response larger than the category she had assigned to it.

And suddenly:

**Oh.**

Not *I have begun to care.*

Rather:

**I was already far more emotionally invested than I had permitted myself to recognise.**

That is much stronger.

Because the Bottle doesn't manufacture the emotion. It **reveals its existing depth**.

And that probably means we need to be disciplined before then. Celeste can show care constantly, but she shouldn't label it too cleanly.

She does things.

She notices.

She intervenes.

She worries in practical language.

She adjusts plans.

She gives Charlie space.

She protects without calling it protection.

She misses him without saying, *I missed him.*

She can even become annoyed because something has disturbed her usual equilibrium, without realising the annoyance is evidence that Charlie has become important enough to disturb it.

Then after the Bottle, the emotional vocabulary can begin changing.

Not overnight into a completely different narrator. That would feel false. But perhaps Celeste starts catching herself.

Earlier Celeste might write:

*Charlie was late. I found myself checking the clock twice.*

Later Celeste might admit:

*Charlie was late. I was worried.*

And much later:

*Charlie was late, and I hated how frightened that made me.*

Same woman.

Increasing access.

That's the slow reveal.

And there's something else rather lovely about it: **the reader may understand Celeste before Celeste understands Celeste.**

They may see the care accumulating.

They may notice how much page-space Charlie occupies in her attention.

They may notice that she remembers tiny things about him.

They may notice her emotional investment through what she chooses to narrate.

Celeste thinks she's telling us about Charlie.

The reader gradually realises:

**Celeste, you're telling us about you.**

That could become one of the quiet pleasures of *Unhidden*.

So yes—I think stripping out the explicit emotional commentary was the right instinct. The risk isn't that Celeste becomes underwritten. The risk would be giving her interiority in the same manner as everybody else and thereby destroying the one character whose mystery is built into the narrative apparatus itself.

Celeste is not mysterious because the author refuses to tell us about her.

She's mysterious because **Celeste refuses—mostly without realising it—to tell us about herself.**

That is much better. 💞

Which is partly why I think our wandering around Wardrobe tonight has been genuinely useful rather than merely distraction. We've moved from that material into a place built around **ordinary care**.

We've found Mara's ridiculous second-hand boardroom table. We've put handbags into pigeonholes. None of those things was manufactured because we sat down and said, *We require some symbolism.*
