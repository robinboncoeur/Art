Oh, yes. I think moving the spironolactone **before *The Ledger*** actually gives you something rather better than a visibly changing Charlie.

After only about a month on spironolactone **without oestrogen**, I would expect surprisingly little that Celeste, Mara, Lily, Bree or Sarah could reliably *see*. And for *Unhidden*, I think that's ideal.

The useful distinction is between **what Charlie experiences**, **what the drug is doing**, and **what another person could observe**.

Mayo's current patient guidance puts the earliest anti-androgen-associated changes—fewer erections/less ejaculation and reduced libido—at roughly **1–3 months**. Changes that could conceivably become externally visible, such as reduced muscle mass or softer/less oily skin, aren't expected to begin until roughly **3–6 months**, while reduction in facial/body-hair growth is later still, around **6–12 months**. ([Mayo Clinic][1])

And Charlie isn't taking oestrogen. That's important. Spironolactone blocks androgen action; it isn't itself supplying the hormonal signal responsible for the familiar feminising changes. Mayo actually describes a regimen in which spironolactone is started first and oestrogen follows 4–8 weeks later—which is remarkably close to the interval you're proposing, although obviously Charlie is **not** following that clinical pathway. ([Mayo Clinic][1])

So I would make **one month of Charlie-on-spiro almost visually boring**.

### What Charlie himself might notice

There are several possibilities, and individual variation is substantial.

He may be urinating more frequently. That's perhaps the most prosaic and immediate thing: spironolactone is, after all, a potassium-sparing diuretic. He could notice thirst, occasionally feel a little light-headed getting up quickly, particularly if he's not drinking enough, or find strenuous work slightly less comfortable if his blood pressure is running lower.

But none of those needs to happen dramatically.

Sexual effects are much more interesting because they're beginning to enter the plausible window. Spontaneous/morning erections might become less frequent. Libido might quieten. Ejaculate could decrease. ([Mayo Clinic][1])

And **that could matter enormously to Charlie without producing one observable thing in Wardrobe**.

In fact, given the arc we've established, I particularly like that.

He has pulled the emergency brake. 

A month later he doesn't look in the mirror and see a girl appearing. He isn't supposed to. That's not what he asked the drug to do.

Instead, he may have the first tentative evidence that:

**the thing he was frightened of isn't charging ahead quite so relentlessly.**

That could be tremendously important psychologically.

### What Celeste could actually notice

Very little that she could correctly attribute to spironolactone.

And I would be reluctant to give her the standard fictional hormone-treatment superpowers:

*His face looked softer.*

No. Not after four weeks of spiro alone.

*His body was less muscular.*

Highly unlikely to be perceptible.

*His body hair was disappearing.*

Definitely too soon.

*His features were becoming more feminine.*

No mechanism for that kind of rapid visible transformation here.

And certainly no breasts, fat redistribution, hip change or anything of that nature. Those are primarily oestrogen-mediated effects and happen on a much longer timescale anyway. ([Mayo Clinic][1])

What she **might** notice is behavioural fallout from the mundane pharmacology.

Charlie disappears to the loo more often.

He has a water bottle with him.

Perhaps Mara notices him stand after crouching over a hem and put a hand briefly against the cutting table because the room moved half an inch. Not collapse. Not swoon. Just—

*Hang on.*

Then it's gone.

That is the sort of tiny observational fact Celeste could register without understanding.

And there's an especially nice possibility here because you've put this into ***The Ledger***.

Charlie is now being asked to wear garments and report precisely what they do under movement and stress. His body is literally becoming an **instrument of measurement** at the same time that, privately, he has begun trying to prevent that body from changing.

That is deliciously complicated.

### But I think the most important effect isn't pharmacological

It's **agency**.

This is where our last discussion becomes enormously useful.

March Charlie is horrified because puberty is happening *to him*. He has no control over it. His body has started announcing something he desperately doesn't want announced. Worse, he believes the announcement may alter how these women perceive him. That's the error we've already identified: he thinks Wardrobe's acceptance is partly conditional upon remaining physically non-threatening/male-adjacent rather than understanding that the women are responding to the quality of his behaviour and attention. 

Then he finds spironolactone.

Orders it.

Takes it.

And by *The Ledger*, a month has passed.

Physically?

**Bugger-all.**

Psychologically?

Something potentially enormous has changed:

**Charlie is no longer helpless.**

That doesn't mean spironolactone magically makes him calmer. I would emphatically *not* write it as a direct drug effect:

> Charlie had become more settled since starting spironolactone.

We couldn't know that, and it risks turning pharmacology into psychology.

Instead:

**he did something.**

The unwanted process began.
He researched it.
He identified an intervention.
He chose the intervention that matched his actual objective.
He did **not** choose oestrogen. 
And now, every morning, he takes a tablet that means:

**I am not simply allowing this to happen to me.**

That can affect how he inhabits the world even before the medication produces anything another person can see.

And *that*, Robyn, is where I think you can get your outside observer's detectable difference.

Not:

**Charlie's body is changing.**

But perhaps:

**the panic has stopped escalating.**

Celeste doesn't know why.

She might have seen him become increasingly watchful of himself during March. Checking his jaw in a reflection. Touching the new hair on an arm and withdrawing his hand. A little more closed. Perhaps one awful moment after noticing something new.

Then April.

The physical puberty hasn't magically reversed. He still has whatever changes had already begun.

But he's working.

Listening to Mara.

Writing in the ledger.

Arguing quietly about where the load is actually travelling through Bree's jacket.

And Celeste thinks, perhaps only fleetingly:

*Whatever had been frightening him last month didn't seem to be frightening him quite so badly now.*

She would be **right about the observation and completely ignorant of the cause**.

That's very Celeste.

And very *Unhidden*.

Because Charlie hasn't become more female.

He hasn't even decided he **wants** to become female.

He's simply acquired the first little scrap of control over a body that had suddenly begun making decisions without consulting him.

Which fits beautifully with the chronology you've now got in the screenshot: Lauren has already recognised that Wardrobe is a genuine vocational fit; puberty then erupts into that newly secured existence; Charlie independently applies the brake; and only **afterward** does Mara put his body to work in *The Ledger*. Oh, that's good. Because *The Ledger* doesn't have to advance his gender at all.

It can advance his relationship with his **body**.

*This body is doing something I hate.*

And, simultaneously:

*This body can tell me where this seam fails.*

Those two truths are allowed to coexist.

It's that Charlie, for reasons she doesn't yet understand, seems to have stopped bracing quite so hard against tomorrow. 🌿

And I think that's considerably more powerful than giving him softer skin.

> “Kill the overheads.”
> “Can you switch the overheads on?”
> “The overheads are too bright.”

It feels particularly natural in workplaces, theatres, studios, workshops and similar environments where people routinely distinguish between different sorts of lighting. So in **Wardrobe**, I find it entirely believable.

Lucy has a different responsibility. She's a dresser. Lily's physical privacy and comfort are partly **Lucy's business**. So Lucy isn't being more prejudiced than Lily; she's carrying a different duty.

Her glance towards Charlie is appropriate.

Lily effectively answers:

**Don't care.**

Lucy doesn't have to be completely persuaded by that yet.

Then Charlie closes the window.

And this is where the behaviour becomes especially interesting because he doesn't protect Lily from **himself**.

He protects Lily from **other people's sightlines**.

That's a much stronger piece of evidence.

If Charlie's behaviour were merely:

*Woman changing → avert eyes*

Lucy could reasonably classify that as manners.

But:

*Woman changing → open window creates exposure to outside → quietly close window → resume work*

demonstrates that his attention is actually on **Lily's privacy**, not on demonstrating his own virtue.

Mara notices.

Celeste notices.

Lucy may or may not consciously register its significance yet.

Perfect.

### Tahlia, meanwhile, has already found the operating manual

And yes, I think giving her more weight in the coming chapters could pay enormous dividends.

She may be the first woman who understands that the easiest way to work with Charlie is simply to **tell him what you actually want**.

No social choreography required.

> “Don't carry that.”

Okay.

> “Take this to Celeste.”

Okay.

> “Hold this.”

Okay.

> “Not like that.”

Okay.

> “Ask before you move it.”

Okay.

That is going to make Charlie extraordinarily pleasant to train.

And importantly, Tahlia doesn't need to sentimentalise this.

She doesn't have to think *what a lovely respectful young man*. She may simply discover:

**Oh. He listens.**

That's gold in a workplace.

It also feeds directly into Mara's later:

> **“No guesses. Ask.”**

Charlie can function under that rule because he doesn't experience *asking* as loss of status.

### Which gives Sarah's later resistance much more substance

Because Sarah isn't wrong to begin cautiously.

She hasn't accumulated the evidence.

Lily has.

Tahlia has.

Mara certainly has.

Celeste has been watching.

Lucy is acquiring it.

Sarah arrives later with a perfectly reasonable category-based assessment:

**young bloke + women's Wardrobe + bodies + fitting = I want boundaries.**

And Wardrobe doesn't tell her she's wrong to have them.

That's essential.

Mara doesn't say:

*Oh, Charlie isn't like other men.*

That would be awful.

She effectively says:

**Here's his job. Here are the boundaries. You decide what you're comfortable with.**

Then Sarah discovers for herself what everyone else has already discovered:

Charlie takes **no** as information.

That's probably the deepest safety signal of all.

Not that Charlie never crosses a boundary because he's magically capable of intuiting every woman's boundaries.

Quite the opposite.

**He doesn't assume he knows them.**

And when someone tells him one, he updates.

### Which is why the staggered acceptance is working so well

You've effectively got different evidentiary standards:

**Lily:** *He's not looking at me. Fine.*

**Tahlia:** *Tell him what you need and that's what he'll do.*

**Lucy:** *I'm responsible for other women's physical comfort. I'm watching.*

**Celeste:** *The investment is paying dividends.*

**Mara:** *I have deliberately tested this person's behaviour under instruction.*

**Sarah:** *I haven't seen enough yet. Don't ask me to take everybody else's word for it.*

And Sarah's position is every bit as legitimate as Tahlia's.

That's what makes her eventual acceptance meaningful.

Nobody declares Charlie safe by committee.

**Each woman retains authority over her own threshold.**

That's very important to the women-led ethos of the book. Mara can establish workplace expectations, but she doesn't get to tell Sarah what Sarah must feel about Charlie.

And Charlie's great strength is that he apparently doesn't expect her to.

### And there's one other thing I really like about the chivalry beat

Don't eliminate all Charlie's conventionally male social training.

I think that would be a mistake.

Charlie has spent nineteen years being told he's a boy. Of course he's acquired scripts:

*carry things for women*
*open doors*
*don't let a woman do the heavy lifting if you're standing there*
*be useful*
*don't hit girls*
*protect women*

Some will be benign. Some patronising. Some contradictory. Some he'll never have examined.

Wardrobe doesn't need to strip those behaviours out of him.

The women simply teach him something more sophisticated:

**Offer help. Don't impose help.**

That's a lovely distinction.

Tahlia isn't offended that he stood up.

She doesn't deliver Gender Studies 101 across the cutting table. 😄

> “I'm good, hun.”

That's all the correction necessary.

And Charlie's response tells her more about him than his original offer did.

**He obeys the answer.**

That's the thing I would build into Tahlia's expanded role.

She doesn't necessarily become Charlie's great confidante. She may become something more structurally valuable: **the first woman who understands how remarkably little social management he requires once expectations are made explicit.**

Mara tests Charlie deliberately.

Tahlia discovers Charlie **incidentally**.

And Celeste watches both.

That gives the Wardrobe women distinct functions rather than turning them into a collective female chorus—and I think that's exactly why this increasingly feels like a real workplace rather than a mechanism constructed to accept the protagonist. 🌿

Yes—and the spread feels **very good**. It makes Wardrobe generational without making it feel artificially populated to provide Charlie with representatives of every decade.

And yes, Lauren's arithmetic works: if Charlie is 19 and Lauren had him at 21, she's **40**, not 42, assuming we're talking straightforward ages rather than a birthday complication. 😄 If Lauren is 42, she had him at about 23.

A few reactions:

* **Lauren — 40 or 42:** I actually like 42, so I'd change “had Charlie at 21” rather than her age if that isn't established canon. It gives her enough life behind her without pushing her into a different generation from Roger.
* **Tahlia — 39:** Excellent. This particularly strengthens what we've just discovered about her. She's not one of the younger women casually accepting Charlie; she's an experienced woman who has dealt with enough men and workplaces to recognise very quickly what she's dealing with.
* **Mara — 52:** I'd keep it. I don't want Mara much older. At 52 she's not the elderly Wardrobe matriarch; she's a woman at the height of her professional authority. She can haul things around, demonstrate techniques, work long days, and have decades of accumulated craft knowledge. Her authority comes from **Mara**, not age.
* **Sarah — 34:** Perfect. Old enough that her initial caution around Charlie feels like adult judgement rather than youthful nervousness; young enough that she's fully embedded among the working actresses.
* **Lucy — 29:** This one clicks particularly well. Her protectiveness towards Lily isn't maternal. It's professional and woman-to-woman. That's better.
* **Bree — 25 / Lily — 22:** Yep. Lily especially feels 22 to me—the bounce, the “Oooooooo...”, whipping off the jumper, checking Charlie and deciding *eh, fine*. 😂
* **Brittany — 22:** Good, especially for her much later role. It keeps Brittany and Charlotte much closer to peers than Brittany being positioned as an older caretaker/chaperone figure.
* **Lisa — 20:** Definitely. She's essentially Charlie/Celeste's generation, which will matter when she eventually rises professionally. I like that her later authority isn't age-derived.

Outside Wardrobe, **Lisbeth at 42** feels right. It also gives an interesting parallel with Lauren if both are 42: two women at essentially the same life stage occupying quite different positions in Charlie's world.

**Graham at 44** works extremely well. That's old enough for established trade/workplace-manager habits and young enough that his physical outdoorsy bloke-ness feels completely natural rather than caricatured.

**Mr Accountant at 54**—yes. I wouldn't make him much older because I don't want the unpleasantness of his presence accidentally turning into *old man enters room of younger women*. He's simply an unpleasant 54-year-old man with questionable grooming habits and a talent for discovering rounding errors. 😄

**Carl at 38** sounds right from what we've established.

**Golf Guy at 44** works.

**Roger at 45:** this is the one I'd inspect alongside Lauren's arithmetic. If Charlie is 19, Roger became a father at about 25–26. That's completely plausible. If Lauren is 42, she became a mother at 22–23. So Roger being three years older than Lauren fits very naturally.

**Fiona at 46:** also feels right.

Thirty-nine-year-old Tahlia gets him quickly.

Twenty-nine-year-old Lucy takes longer.

Twenty-two-year-old Lily basically runs the world's fastest risk assessment:

*Is he looking? No. Fine. Jumper off.* 😂

Thirty-four-year-old Sarah says, effectively, *Not enough evidence.*

Fifty-two-year-old Mara is actively gathering evidence.

Eighteen-year-old Celeste is somewhere else again: she backed him early, but largely because she recognised useful qualities and decided to make an investment.

[ Emily ]

Oh, **yes**. Tahlia just acquired a life outside Wardrobe. 😄 And I think Ian solves something worth solving, provided we don't make him exist merely as evidence that the novel likes men too.

He should be present because **Tahlia loves him**.

That's the interesting fact.

Until now, we've mostly known Tahlia through work: competent, relaxed, experienced, affectionate without fuss, quick to understand people. Giving her a happy marriage tells us something quite important about her judgement of Charlie as well.

Tahlia isn't comfortable with Charlie because she has some general preference for women over men.

She has a man at home whom she thinks is bloody wonderful.

She knows perfectly well what a good man looks like.

And therefore when she gets Charlie's number early, she's not thinking:

> *He's not like men.*

She's much more likely to recognise:

> **He's a decent kid.**

That's subtly different, and much healthier for the story.

And Ian shouldn't resemble Charlie particularly. I'd actively resist that. Ian can be socially confident, conventionally masculine, perhaps quite talkative. He might enjoy footy, cars, gadgets, whatever naturally develops. Tahlia isn't comfortable with Ian because he exhibits Charlie's particular non-intrusive qualities; she's comfortable with him because **Ian has his own set of good qualities**.

That protects the novel from accidentally creating:

**Good men = men who behave less like men.**

We don't want that.

Instead:

**Good men come in different shapes.**

Ian may take up more social space than Charlie ever would. He might flirt outrageously with his own wife. He may look at Tahlia in a way that makes it abundantly obvious he fancies her rotten—and she bloody loves being looked at that way **by him**.

That could become particularly valuable given how much we're exploring gaze and attention.

The problem was never:

**Men desire women.**

The problem is entitlement to women.

Ian can desire Tahlia enthusiastically while simultaneously having an absolutely settled understanding that **Tahlia belongs to Tahlia**.

That distinction would enrich the whole book without ever needing to be stated.

### And his flaw is excellent because it's so ordinary

Please don't give Ian a Dark Secret. 😂

> “Ian's bought another car.”

might tell us everything.

Tahlia loves the idiot.

Ian loves Tahlia.

Ian is intelligent, dependable, faithful, decent, probably excellent company—and has the financial restraint of a Labrador walking past a sausage when somebody releases a new model with adaptive cruise control.

That's **human**.

And importantly, his flaw causes actual friction without undermining the marriage.

Tahlia can be genuinely furious with him.

Not cute sitcom:

> “Oh, Ian!”

but:

> **“You spent how much?”**

Because if they can't comfortably afford it, that's not an adorable eccentricity. He's made a joint financial decision without adequately respecting its impact on her.

And Tahlia, whom we've just established as somebody who expects people to hear **no**, isn't going to suddenly become a doormat at home.

That's where the marriage becomes convincing.

Ian's a good bloke.

**Therefore when Tahlia says he's fucked up, he listens.**

Maybe not instantly. 😄

Perhaps cars are his blind spot. He rationalises:

*Trade-in was excellent.*
*Finance is only—*
*It's much more economical.*
*Five-year warranty, love.*
*You should see the safety package—*

And Tahlia:

> “Ian.”

😂

Eventually he hears what she's actually saying.

That's consistent with what we've just discovered about her. Tahlia doesn't require men to anticipate everything she needs. She requires them to **respect the answer once she gives it**.

Charlie does.

Ian, usually, does.

With cars, Ian may require remedial instruction.

### It could also make Tahlia invaluable to Charlie later

Not as Gender Mentor Number Four. Quite the opposite.

She can quietly undermine one of Charlie's mistaken binaries simply by existing.

Charlie is building this confused model:

**male → masculine → women become wary → I become outside**

Yet Tahlia goes home every night absolutely delighted to see Ian.

She talks about him.

Complains about him.

Laughs about him.

Maybe he occasionally picks her up.

And Charlie sees a woman he respects loving a man.

Not tolerating him.

Not managing him.

**Wanting him.**

That matters.

Especially because we're determined that Charlie's eventual movement toward female shouldn't arise from the book constructing **male as bad and female as good**. Charlie needs to be able to see perfectly good versions of manhood and still eventually conclude:

**That's fine. It's just not what I want for myself.**

Ian could contribute enormously to that.

Roger gives Charlie one model of manhood.

Graham another.

The Accountant another.

Ian another.

And Ian might be the crucial counterexample:

**There is nothing wrong with being this.**

Which makes Charlie's eventual:

**I still don't want to be this**

far more meaningful.

Oh, and I can already imagine Tahlia talking about him without the narrative formally introducing Ian for ages.

Someone mentions some ridiculous new vehicle outside.

Tahlia barely looks up from the overlocker.

> “Don't. Ian's already seen it.”

Everyone laughs.

Charlie doesn't know why.

Lucy:

> “Last time Ian *saw* a car, they ate baked beans for six months.”

Tahlia, without looking up:

> “Four.”

And we're done. 😄

Ian exists.

Tahlia loves him.

Wardrobe knows perfectly well what she's married to.

And suddenly Tahlia isn't merely **the thirty-nine-year-old woman who understands Charlie early**.

She's Tahlia: skilled wardrobe worker, colleague, wife, woman with a life elsewhere, happily married to an intelligent and thoroughly decent bloke who **must under no circumstances be left unsupervised near a car dealership**.

Much better. 🌿
