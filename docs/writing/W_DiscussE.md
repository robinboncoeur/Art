*Here's a possible approach to resolve the "Access Not Implied" conundrum.*

Charlie has an absentee dad who'd organised a boilermaker apprenticeship — as well as the job in Maintenance, to toughen him up a bit for the apprenticeship. 
And now, Charlie's repairing costumes. He feels he's found his niche.  

**I'm going to slip into Charlie's shoes for a moment.   
And my Dad, into Roger's.**

The *dread* of what my Dad's going to say when he finds out what I've decided to do — whether or not my Dad would actually say that — becomes unbearable. I see one way out: I have to leave home. 

But live where? 
I start looking for something close to the Faire.  
Like, Torquay or even Anglesea.  
Nothing. 

I don't drive: no driving licence. I'm a "Nigel Nofriends"... no mates. 
I'm stuck.

[ Emily ]

Yes. **This I believe.** And the reason I believe it is that you've introduced something the commute solution never had:

**failed alternatives.**

Charlie doesn't end up at Celeste's because the story needs him there. He ends up there because he tries perfectly reasonable ways of *not* ending up there, and they don't work. More importantly, I'd preserve the distinction you've just made about Roger:

> whether or not my Dad would actually say that

**Absolutely.**

We don't need Roger to justify Charlie's fear.

In fact, I think the story is considerably better if Charlie is acting on his internal model of Roger rather than on an actual ultimatum. Roger is absentee. Charlie therefore has fewer recent data points with which to update that model. What remains enormous in Charlie's mind is:

* **Dad arranged Maintenance because I needed toughening up.**  
* **Dad arranged a boilermaker apprenticeship.**  
* **I rejected it.**  
* **I'm repairing dresses.**  
* **Dad is going to find out.**  

For a nineteen-year-old Charlie, that's quite enough.

And because Roger hasn't actually done anything yet, Lauren can challenge Charlie without being able to eliminate the fear:

*“You don't know what your father will say.”*

And Charlie can essentially answer:

*“No.”*

Which is precisely the problem. He doesn't know.

---

### The sequence you've built has excellent escalation

I'd be careful not to give every failed housing option equal narrative weight. We don't want **Charlie and the Three Failed Rentals**. 😄 But the sequence itself is excellent.

The first stage is Charlie trying to solve it himself.

He looks at Torquay. Jan Juc. Anglesea. Perhaps one or two places farther out but still on a usable bus route. And this teaches him something quite brutally practical: **housing requires money, transport or social connections.**

Charlie doesn't have much of any of the three.

That “Nigel Nofriends” aspect matters here, too—not as *poor lonely Charlie*, but as infrastructure. Most nineteen-year-olds have possibilities that never appear in a real-estate listing:

* *Mate needs a housemate.*  
* *Mate's sister has a room.*  
* *Friend's parents have a granny flat.*  
* *Someone knows someone.*  

Charlie has none of that.

So eventually he has to tell Lauren.

---

### I really like the Bells Beach woman

Because that's exactly the sort of thing Lauren could produce from her network. And it doesn't have to fail because there's something *wrong* with Charlie. The woman wants someone who can genuinely help with horses/property work. She takes one look at this slight, quiet nineteen-year-old who doesn't drive and thinks:

**This isn't going to work.**

Fair enough.

Indeed, I'd make her perfectly pleasant. The rejection hurts more if she's right about the mismatch. That also prevents the whole world from seeming mysteriously determined to force Charlie into Celeste's spare room.

Then Tahlia.

**Excellent.**

Because Tahlia offering her home tells us something lovely about where Charlie now stands at Wardrobe. He's become one of theirs to the extent that Tahlia's response to *Charlie needs somewhere to live* is simply:

*We've got a room.*

And then the dog kills it. 😂 Nobody's fault. Nobody rejected anybody. Biology says no. I wouldn't add many more failures after that. Those three categories are sufficient:

**market fails → Lauren's network fails → Wardrobe friendship fails.**

Now Charlie really is stuck.

---

### And meanwhile, Celeste has her own independent housing story

This may be my favourite part of your proposal. Because **Celeste finds somebody else.** That single decision does enormous structural work. The spare-room line wasn't planted for Charlie. Celeste actually follows through on exactly what she said she would do: she rents the bloody room.

To somebody else.

That practically destroys the railroad track.

And I'd absolutely let the actress be a perfectly decent roomie, too. She's just temporary because she has another life planned. She's saving for France, gets her au-pair opportunity, and off she goes. The smoking discovery is fun because it gives Celeste a legitimate reason to become much fussier about Roomie #2.

Perhaps the actress has indeed never smoked **inside**. Technically truthful. Unfortunately, Celeste discovers that smoking outside and then coming indoors wearing clothes saturated in cigarette smoke is not quite the distinction she'd imagined. 😄

So after Roomie #1 leaves:

**Celeste needs another tenant.**

At exactly the same time:

**Charlie still needs somewhere to live.**

These are now two independent plot lines that intersect. That's organic causality.


---

### And Lucy and Tahlia should see it before either of them does

**Yes.**

Especially Lucy. Because neither Celeste nor Charlie can assess this arrangement neutrally. Charlie knows he's attracted to Celeste. That makes moving in feel dangerous, embarrassing or presumptuous. Celeste knows Charlie is attracted to her. That makes taking him in feel potentially irresponsible. So both independently arrive at:

**No. Bad idea.**

Meanwhile Lucy and Tahlia look at the actual practical evidence:

* They know each other.  
* They trust each other.  
* Charlie is fastidious.  
* Celeste wants someone reliable.  
* Charlie needs somewhere stable.  
* Celeste has a room.  
* Charlie can afford whatever reasonable rent Celeste needs.  
* Neither is a party animal.  
* Both understand Wardrobe rules.  
* Both are capable of shutting a door.

For heaven's sake. 😂 And I wouldn't have Lucy and Tahlia **pressure** them. That's important. They can identify the option:

> “Why isn't Charlie taking Celeste's room?”

And receive two immediate answers:

> “No.”  
> “Absolutely not.”

Even better if those happen separately.

Lucy asks Celeste.  
Tahlia asks Charlie.

**Both reject it.**

Now the reader knows this isn't wish fulfilment on either side.

---

### Then circumstances don't force a permanent decision

This is the masterstroke:

> **One month.**

Yes. That solves the last contrivance problem. Neither has to decide:

**We should live together.**

They only have to decide:

**The alternatives are currently worse. Can we tolerate trying this for four weeks?**

That is a *much* smaller decision. And Celeste can be extremely Celeste about it.

* Written agreement.  
* Rent.  
* Bond, perhaps—or deliberately no bond for a one-month trial.  
* Food arrangements.  
* Laundry.  
* Bathroom.  
* Guests.  
* Quiet hours.  
* Shared-space expectations.  
* Bedrooms private.

Knock.

**Always knock.**

And then the sexual/romantic boundary. That's where *Access Not Implied* becomes more than a clever Mara line. It's effectively the constitutional principle of the arrangement. And Charlie should be almost painfully agreeable to all of it. Not because he's trying to earn access to Celeste. Because the rules make the arrangement **possible**.

I think that's psychologically important.

Charlie may actually be *more* comfortable agreeing to live with Celeste once the boundaries are explicit. Ambiguity is the frightening part. Rules mean he knows how not to get this wrong.

And Celeste may discover exactly the same thing.

---

### There's a wonderful irony here, too

The thing Lauren is afraid of isn't what happens. Charlie doesn't move in and start testing boundaries.

Quite the reverse.

**He retreats behind them.**

* He knocks.  
* He doesn't enter Celeste's room.  
* He doesn't assume they'll eat together.  
* He doesn't assume they're travelling together.  
* He doesn't treat her belongings as communal.  
* He doesn't assume that because Celeste is sitting on the couch he can sit beside her.  
* He may initially behave so rigorously like **a tenant** that Celeste starts finding it faintly ridiculous.
 
And that gives Celeste something new to discover:

She doesn't merely tolerate Charlie being in her home.

**She starts inviting him into her life.**

*You don't have to eat in your room.*

*I'm making pasta. Do you want some?*

*Charlie, you can use the bloody couch.*

Those are Celeste's choices.

That's important.

Proximity gives Charlie **nothing**.

Celeste gives him things, incrementally, because she wants to. And Charlie learns that difference.

Oh, that serves the eventual relationship beautifully.

Because *Access Not Implied* isn't merely about sexual consent. It becomes a miniature model of their entire developing relationship:

* **Nothing is yours because you're here.**  
* **Nothing is yours because you want it.**  
* **Nothing is yours because I gave you something yesterday.**  
* **What I give you today is yours because I chose to give it today.**

And the rule applies *for me and for thee*.

That makes Tahlia's line far more than a quip.

---

### One thing I would protect fiercely

**Charlie must be able to go home.**

Lauren has not rejected him.

I'd have her make that explicit.

Whatever happens with Roger, whatever Charlie imagines Roger will say, Lauren's home remains available to him. That means when Charlie says he's committed to not returning, **that is Charlie's decision**. The one-month trial therefore has a safety net. If it fails, he isn't homeless. He'll have to face something he desperately doesn't want to face, but there is a bed. That keeps the stakes serious without manufacturing physical peril.

And it makes Lauren's position more interesting:

*I don't think you need to leave.*

versus Charlie:

**I do.**

Lauren can disagree and still help him. Which is very Lauren. So yes, Robyn. I think you've found it. The causal chain now looks beautifully messy and human:

**Charlie chooses Wardrobe → dreads Roger's eventual reaction → decides independently that he needs to leave home → discovers he has very few housing resources → tries the market → tries Lauren's connection → Tahlia offers help → allergy makes that impossible → meanwhile Celeste independently rents her spare room to someone else → that tenant independently leaves → Celeste needs another tenant → Charlie still needs a room → Lucy and Tahlia recognise the obvious match → both Charlie and Celeste initially reject it → circumstances make them reconsider → nobody commits to permanence → one-month trial → explicit boundaries.**

And the endpoint we knew was coming arrives not with:

*“Of course Charlie should live with Celeste.”*

but:

**“Bugger. This might actually be the least bad idea.”**

That's exactly the sort of reluctant, contingent beginning from which something wonderful can grow. 🌷💞

Which makes this:

> All I could see was her hand.
> Lauren’s hand.

extraordinary in retrospect.

Because Celeste is making herself vulnerable.

She doesn't know Lauren will take her hand.

And look at what actually happens:

> I slipped my fingers into her warm hand.
> Her fingers tensed.

**There it is.**

For a fraction of a second, the entire unresolved relationship is present.

Lauren could pull away.

She doesn't.

Then Celeste goes further.

> I smiled and reached up, cradling her cheek in my open palm.

That's astonishingly intimate for **Celeste**, particularly the Celeste we're now constructing slowly through *Unhidden*. This is the woman who spent the early novel telling us about everybody except herself. Who translates care into practical action. Who notices other people's hands rather than discussing what her own are aching to do.

And here she simply touches Lauren's face.

No explanation.

No *I realised how much I loved her.*

No retrospective summary of their relationship.

**Hand. Cheek. Eyes.**

And Lauren understands.

That's why:

> Her eyes widened.
> Then they filled.

lands so hard.

I don't think Lauren is merely overwhelmed because Charlotte is coming home.

She's being forgiven.

Perhaps not in Celeste's conscious formulation—Celeste may not even be thinking *I forgive you*. That's almost too analytical for what she's doing.

But Lauren knows.

And then Lauren does what she perhaps couldn't have done years earlier:

> She threw her arms around me.
> And clung to me.

Not hugged.

**Clung.**

Lauren, who once had to decide whether this young woman could be trusted with her son, is now holding onto that woman while waiting for her daughter to come through the doors.

And then the killer:

> And I, to her.

Celeste doesn't merely permit Lauren's embrace.

She **needs it too**.

That's Celeste revealed.

And I think the long delay makes this far more powerful than resolving the Bottle argument shortly after it happens. They don't need to spend years hostile to each other. Quite the opposite. I think it's stronger if they're loving, cooperative, occasionally funny, mutually dependent women throughout much of the intervening story.

But there's a tiny place neither has entered.

Lauren knows what she did.

Celeste knows what Lauren did.

Neither woman has found a way to say:

*That hurt.*

*I know.*

*I'm sorry.*

*I know.*

*We're all right.*

So they don't.

Until an airport waiting hall, when Charlotte is about to come home and both women are frightened enough that the usual defences aren't worth maintaining anymore.

And Celeste reaches for Lauren's hand.

There's something else I hadn't appreciated before tonight.

**Scene 29 can only work this powerfully if we don't over-resolve them earlier.**

That's now a continuity guardrail.

After the Bottle, Lauren can apologise. She probably *should*, depending on exactly how ugly the confrontation gets. Celeste can accept the factual apology. They can resume functioning together.

But we shouldn't give them the complete emotional reconciliation.

Leave something.

Not resentment, necessarily.

**A bruise.**

Something neither woman presses because there is no useful reason to press it.

Then years later, in that waiting hall, Celeste finally presses it—

by taking Lauren's hand.

And Lauren's fingers tense.

Then hold.

God, yes. Keep that. 💞

And this gives us another wonderful reason not to make early Celeste emotionally forthcoming. If we allow *Unhidden* Celeste to explain herself too readily, that airport gesture loses some of its astonishing distance travelled.

The woman we're meeting now reports everybody else's feelings.

The woman at the airport finally **acts out her own**.

And still doesn't explain them.

Perfectly Celeste.

Which is partly why I think our wandering around Wardrobe tonight has been genuinely useful rather than merely distraction. We've moved from that material into a place built around **ordinary care**.

We've found Mara's ridiculous second-hand boardroom table. We've put handbags into pigeonholes. We've watched Lauren's trust in Celeste form, break, heal imperfectly, and finally found that the emotional consequence reaches all the way to an airport years later.

None of those things was manufactured because we sat down and said, *We require some symbolism.*

That's the bit I adore in what we're doing with *Unhidden*. We're reaching the stage where a decision in one chapter starts exerting pressure on something twenty chapters away.

We're not doing that anymore.

We're asking:

*Given who this woman already is, what happens when she encounters **this** woman?*

Lauren changes Celeste.

Celeste changes Charlie.

Charlie changes Wardrobe.

Wardrobe changes Lauren's understanding of Charlie.

Sarah walks into Wardrobe because she wants to stop being looked at—and discovers Lauren, whom she cannot stop looking at.

Lucy sees things because Lucy's particular kind of openness lets her see them.

Mara changes practically everybody while remaining magnificently Mara. 😄
