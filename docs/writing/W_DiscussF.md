[ Robyn ]

An update for you, Emily (or a recap, if you refer). 

In 'No Room', we left Charlie wanting to leave home, but with no place to move to.

Then, in 'The Missing Spark', Sarah realises her future is woman-shaped.

And now, things come to a head for Lauren. Roger's returning home after a long stint on the Naval Base. So, this chapter 'No Longer Needed' (title totally up for discussion, perhaps a bit obvious) needs to do several key things:

- Lauren confronts Roger about his affair
- he's incensed that she's going through his messages, sidestepping his infidelity completely
- Lauren suddenly realises the relationship has been done for a long time, sees no reason to continue the charade
- Roger realises Lauren's serious, starts having a tantrum
- Lauren appreciates she might be in danger of violence
- Sarah messages her, tells her to come to her place 
- Lauren decides to leave

There are some big holes in this chapter. 

1. Just because "I hadn’t told Sarah anything, but I noticed her eyes on me all day." isn't enough of a reason she should message Lauren just then. That needs dealing with.
2. What about Charlie? Lauren is a mother first. She would stay because Charlie lives there too, to protect her son. If she left and Roger — now furious over Lauren leaving and Charlie's career choices... yeah, that doesn't wash.

Got some thinking to do.

Here's what I have so far:

# Edits

[ *Lauren* ]

Wardrobe had always been busy in the way a good kitchen was busy — productive noise, purposeful movement, women speaking in shorthand because competence didn’t need speeches.

But that week was different.

That week the workroom had a weight.

The thirty-six costume order was well underway. Tasks had been allocated. Mara had evening interviewed — albeit reluctantly — some potential applicants for casual staff. Only two had any of the skills Wardrobe needed.

My hands stayed steady on my clipboard as if my body didn’t realise my life was rearranging itself by the hour. I moved through the workroom collecting fabric quotes, noting quantities, listening to Celeste’s quiet directives — this, then this, then this, and all the while, another storm loomed at the horizon of my mind.

Roger.

Coming home after an extended period on the base. It was almost laughable how long I’d managed to put dealing with him on hold by staying too busy to feel anything properly.

But busy-ness had limits.

So did denial.

That afternoon, when I finally went home, the air in my house felt wrong: empty, staged, like a hotel room with painted cinder-block walls displaying local artwork in an effort to look cozy.

Roger’s shoes were by the door. His keys were in the bowl. His scent — aftershave and entitlement — hung in the hallway as if it owned the place.

My stomach tightened.

I didn’t go to the kitchen.

I didn’t tidy. 

I didn’t do any of my old rituals that had served as delay tactics for years.

I went straight to the study.

Roger was there, leaning back in his chair, phone in hand, smiling at something on the screen. He looked up as if he’d been expecting me.

Not with warmth: with calculation.

“Hey.”

I heard my own voice come out calm.

“So, I *know*.”

His smile faltered. Then returned, thinner. “Know… what?”

I placed my handbag on the desk with care. I needed my hands to do something controlled.

“Your messages to—.” I waved my hand, dismissive. “Hotel receipts. The lies that don’t even bother trying anymore.”

His expression did the small, practiced shift into indignation.

“You went through my—"

“Don’t bother.”

It came out as a single word. It surprised me how cleanly it came out. Roger stared at me, recalibrating. 

Then he tried the other tack, the one that used to work.

“Look, you’re tired,” he said. “You’ve been working too much. And you’re letting those women get in your head.”

It hit me that the penny hadn't dropped. He was speaking to the old version of me: the woman who needed him, who stayed because there was no way out.

“No,” I said. “I’m seeing reality for what it is. For the first time in years.”

His jaw tightened. “So what does this mean?”

“It means I’m done.”

“You’re leaving me?”

I paused.

It was strange. 

I was standing in my own house, looking at the man I’d built my life around, and felt nothing but a tired sadness.

“Normally I’d ask you to,” I said. “At your earliest convenience. Tonight.”

His eyes widened, genuinely offended.

“You can’t be serious. You need me!”

Something steady rose in me — quiet, not dramatic.

“No,” I said. “That I don’t. At all.”

I watched that land. Watched the way his face changed — not grief or remorse — just anger at losing a convenience.

“You ungrateful—" he started.

I lifted my hand.

“Don’t.”

Roger stood so suddenly his chair scraped the floor.

“Oh, you think you’re all "independent woman" now, do you?” he snapped. “Just because you’re in some little women’s club playing dress-ups?”

He was so transparent, his words — so revealing.

*Dress-ups.*

“No,” I said. “I’m awake. And I want better than— this.”

He stepped closer. His voice dropped into that intimate threat men used when they wanted you to remember you were smaller.

“You’re making a mistake.”

I didn’t step back.

“Actually, I’m correcting one.”

He stared at me, breathing hard, then turned away with a violent gesture.

“You are *so* going to regret this!”

It was almost a script line. 

It wasn't until after he'd stormed out that my hands started to shake. I stood alone in the strange quiet.

I felt free, but not safe.

Then, as if on cue, my phone buzzed in my pocket.

Sarah.

**You okay?**

My eyes stung.

I hadn’t told Sarah anything, but I noticed her eyes on me all day.

My fingers hovered over the keyboard. I tried to type 'I’m fine'. Instead:

**No**

The reply came instantly, as if she’d been anticipating that response.

**Come here now**

I sat down slowly on the edge of the chair Roger had scraped the floor with. My chest tightened so hard it stole the air from me.

Sarah’s flat certainty shouldn’t have made me cry.

But it did.

My screen was blurry: I found it hard to type.

**I can’t**

A beat. Then:

**Because you’re proud? Or because you’re scared?**

I stared at the words.

My fingers went cold.

I hadn’t said it, not to anyone. I hadn't admitted it even to myself But the true answer rose immediately, humiliating in its honesty:

*I had feelings for Sarah.*

Because, one day recently — I couldn’t tell the exact day — I’d started wanting Sarah’s attention the way you wanted warmth when you’d been cold for too long: quietly, desperately, and with a shame that didn’t belong to me. 

And I was certain Sarah could never want me back.

Sarah was fire. Sarah was sharp. Sarah was the sort of woman who looked like she’d never needed anyone.

And I was— me. 

Middle-aged. 

Careful. 

And, a woman.

My thumbs shook as I typed:

**I don’t think you understand**

Sarah’s reply took slightly longer this time.

**I’m offering you a door that locks Lauren**

I swallowed.

The silence was broken with the sound of Roger's tantrum' — cupboards being slammed, something thrown, the heavy-footed performance of a man making sure I heard him. 

My body cringed before my mind could stop it.

And that decided it.

I typed:

**Ok**

A final message came back, firm as a hand at my elbow.

**Bring nothing you can’t live without. I’ll make tea.**

I stared at the words.

*Tea.*

Such an ordinary promise.

Such an enormous mercy.

I stood, wiped my cheeks with the heel of my hand, and went to the bedroom to pack with the quiet efficiency of a woman who had finally stopped asking permission. At the front door, I hesitated: one last, stupid reflex of loyalty toward a life that hadn’t been loyal to me.

I stepped out.

The evening air was warm and alive. Somewhere a neighbour’s sprinkler hissed. A couple of crows were gossiping on a power pole.

I moved towards my car.

And for the first time that day I felt the faintest hint of peace: not because everything was resolved, but because I knew women who would not let me face it alone.

[ Emily ]

Oh, **yes**. There are holes — but I think they are the useful sort, because the two biggest ones may actually solve each other.

And I think there is a third issue hiding underneath them:

**this chapter currently knows too clearly what it is about.**

Lauren knows the marriage is dead, knows Roger is dangerous, knows she has feelings for Sarah, knows Sarah is sanctuary, packs, leaves, and finishes by understanding the solidarity of women. It is emotionally satisfying, but compared with what we've been doing elsewhere, it's doing rather a lot of the reader's work for them.

I think we can make it considerably more dangerous, quieter, and better.

### First: Charlie is not an obstacle. Charlie is the mechanism.

You've put your finger on the fatal problem with the current ending. Lauren absolutely **cannot** simply walk out while Charlie remains in that house with an enraged Roger.

Not this Lauren.

And especially not when Roger's two grievances have now collided:

**Lauren has rejected him. Charlie has rejected the future Roger arranged for him.**

Roger has lost control of both wife and son on the same evening.

Lauren would see that.

Which suggests to me that her decision isn't:

> *I have to get out.*

It's:

> **Neither of us is staying here tonight.**

And suddenly *No Room* pays off.

Charlie has spent a whole chapter trying and failing to solve his accommodation problem. **Don't solve it there.** Let Lauren's crisis solve it here.

That's deliciously structural.

Lauren doesn't need to decide Charlie's permanent accommodation tonight. She only needs to make one immediate maternal decision:

**Charlie is not coming home.**

Where is Charlie while this confrontation happens?

I think the cleanest answer is almost absurdly simple:

**on the bus home from Wardrobe.**

That commute we've established becomes useful at last.

Lauren gets home before him because she's driving. Roger is already there. The confrontation happens. Roger's behaviour changes. Lauren realises she doesn't feel safe.

And then — *Charlie.*

Not as an authorial problem. As Lauren's first thought.

She grabs her phone.

Calls him.

He's perhaps twenty/thirty/forty minutes away, sitting on a bus, messenger bag beside him, completely unaware.

> “Mum?”
>
> “Where are you?”
>
> “On the bus.”
>
> “Where?”
>
> He tells her.
>
> “Don't come home.”
>
> Silence.
>
> “Mum?”
>
> “Charlie. Listen to me. Don't come home.”

**There's your chapter.**

Because Charlie will understand far more from Lauren's voice than from whatever explanation she gives him.

And now Lauren needs somewhere for **Charlie** to go.

Not herself.

Mother first.

That is when she calls — or messages — Celeste.

Not Sarah.

And I think this is how we finally cash the cheque written in the thirty-six-costume meeting:

> “If Charlie moved closer, like, with Celeste—”

The idea already exists in the social world of the story. Celeste has already said *found a roomie*. The reader doesn't need a new mechanism.

Lauren can call Celeste:

> “I need a favour.”
>
> “Okay.”
>
> “Can Charlie stay with you tonight?”
>
> “Yes.”

I wouldn't even let Celeste ask why.

**Especially** wouldn't let her ask why.

That would be Celeste demonstrating exactly what Lauren needs: *yes first; explanation when you're ready.*

And notice what that does to Lauren emotionally.

She has secured Charlie.

**Now she is allowed to think about herself.**

That feels completely true to her.

---

### And that solves Sarah's message too.

I wouldn't have Sarah magically detect a domestic crisis from meaningful looks across Wardrobe.

Give her **one concrete piece of information** earlier that day.

Lauren doesn't need to confide the affair. She only has to mention:

> “Roger gets home today.”

Sarah knows enough about Roger already — particularly the Maintenance/apprenticeship business and Lauren's reactions whenever he comes up — for that to mean something.

Perhaps Sarah is beside her while they're working:

> “Roger gets back tonight, doesn't he?”
>
> Lauren keeps writing. “Apparently.”
>
> Sarah looks at her.
>
> “What?”
>
> “Nothing.”

That's enough.

Or stronger — and I like this better — Sarah makes a tiny offer before Lauren leaves Wardrobe:

> “Message me when you get home.”
>
> Lauren looks at her. “Why?”
>
> Sarah shrugs. “So I know you got home.”

Lauren knows perfectly well that isn't why.

Sarah knows Lauren knows.

**Neither says it.**

Now the later:

> **You okay?**

isn't psychic Sarah.

It's Sarah doing exactly what she telegraphed she was going to do.

And if Lauren hasn't messaged her by the expected time, Sarah has an even better reason to initiate:

> **You home?**

Lauren:

> **Yes**

Sarah:

> **You okay?**

Lauren stares at it.

> **No**

Oh, I like that much more.

Sarah has not magically diagnosed violence. She has simply opened the door.

Lauren walks through it.

---

There is another thing I'd change fairly substantially.

I don't think this is the place for:

> *I had feelings for Sarah.*

In fact, I think that's the equivalent of the *future shaped like him* line we just murdered in *The Missing Spark*. 😄

Look at what you've already written around it:

> Because, one day recently — I couldn’t tell the exact day — I’d started wanting Sarah’s attention the way you wanted warmth when you’d been cold for too long...

That's lovely prose.

**And I think it should go.**

Because the scene doesn't need Lauren to diagnose her feelings.

Sarah says:

> **Come here now**

Lauren replies:

> **I can't**

Then Sarah:

> **Because you're proud? Or because you're scared?**

But at present Lauren interprets *scared* as somehow connected with her attraction to Sarah, whereas the actual danger in the scene is **Roger**. That muddles the emotional logic precisely when it should sharpen.

I'd make Sarah mean exactly what she appears to mean:

**Are you refusing because accepting help is difficult, or because you're frightened of what Roger will do if you leave?**

And Lauren knows the answer.

That's much stronger.

Something like:

> **Because you're proud? Or because you're scared?**
>
> I stared at the words.
>
> From somewhere down the hall came the crack of a cupboard door.
>
> Then another.
>
> I looked towards the study.
>
> **Scared**
>
> Three dots appeared almost immediately.
>
> **Then this isn't a discussion. Come here.**

Sarah doesn't need to know Lauren loves her.

Lauren doesn't need to know Sarah wants her.

The reader has just come from *The Missing Spark*, where **Sarah** has discovered something enormous.

That gives this scene gorgeous dramatic asymmetry.

**Sarah knows something Lauren doesn't.**

Don't equalise them yet.

Sarah's *Come here* means considerably more to the reader than it means to Lauren.

That is much tastier. 🌷

---

And Roger himself could become more frightening by becoming **less theatrically frightening**.

This:

> “Oh, you think you’re all "independent woman" now, do you?”
> “You are *so* going to regret this!”

is starting to edge towards Villain Roger.

We don't need him to become a monster to justify Lauren leaving him.

Indeed, I think the affair confrontation gives you something nastier and more revealing:

**he simply refuses to discuss the affair.**

Lauren says hotel receipts, messages, lies.

Roger says:

> “You went through my phone?”

And keeps returning to that.

Lauren eventually realises:

**He hasn't denied it. Not once.**

That could be the penny-drop.

Not some magnificent feminist awakening.

Just:

> I waited.
>
> He didn't say it hadn't happened.
>
> He didn't ask what I'd seen.
>
> He didn't even look embarrassed.
>
> He was angry because I'd found it.

*Oh.*

That could be the moment the marriage dies — or, more accurately, the moment Lauren realises it died years ago.

And Roger doesn't need to threaten her explicitly.

He stands.

Chair scrapes.

He gets too close.

Lauren becomes aware of the desk behind her.

That's enough.

No:

> *His voice dropped into that intimate threat men used...*

That is author interpretation — precisely the sort of moral regurgitation we've been happily stripping out.

Instead:

> Roger stood.
>
> The chair shot backwards and struck the filing cabinet.
>
> I didn't move.
>
> He came around the desk.
>
> Only then did I notice I'd put myself between him and the wall.

**There's the danger.**

Lauren's a nurse. She's spent a lifetime reading bodies. Let her body read Roger before her conscious mind catches up.

And perhaps *that* is what changes:

She doesn't think *Roger is going to hit me.*

She thinks:

> *I couldn't get past him.*

That is a frighteningly practical thought.

And once she's had it, she cannot un-have it.

---

Which leads to another important change.

I don't think Roger should storm out.

**Lauren should leave while Roger is still there.**

The current version lets the pressure out:

> It wasn't until after he'd stormed out...

If he's gone, Lauren is safe. The urgency collapses.

Instead, Roger storms **out of the study** and starts banging around elsewhere in the house. That seems to be what the later draft is trying to do anyway, because presently he apparently storms out and then mysteriously continues slamming cupboards inside the house. 😄

Make the geography explicit.

He leaves the room.

Lauren remains.

Then the cupboard.

Another.

Something hits the sink/floor/wall.

She hears him moving through **her house**.

Now:

> *I felt free, but not safe.*

lands beautifully.

And **then Charlie occurs to her.**

I'd actually put Charlie before Sarah's message.

Something structurally like:

1. Lauren confronts Roger.
2. Roger makes the privacy violation the offence; never denies the affair.
3. Lauren realises the marriage isn't merely failing — there's nothing left she wants to save.
4. She tells him it's over.
5. His behaviour changes enough that Lauren becomes physically aware of danger.
6. Roger leaves the study but remains in the house, angrily making his presence felt.
7. Lauren thinks: **Charlie.**
8. Calls Charlie on the bus: *Don't come home.*
9. Calls Celeste: *Can he stay with you tonight?* — immediate yes.
10. **Only after her son is safe** does Lauren discover that she herself has nowhere to go.
11. Sarah, who knew Roger was coming home and had told Lauren to message, checks in.
12. Lauren finally answers **No**.
13. Sarah offers the locked door.
14. Lauren leaves.

That is so much stronger to me.

And it produces a lovely double movement:

**Charlie has been looking for somewhere to leave to.
Lauren hasn't realised she needs somewhere to leave to.**

On the same night, both problems become unavoidable.

---

One more thing: I'd seriously reconsider the final paragraph.

> *...because I knew women who would not let me face it alone.*

It's true.

Which is exactly why Lauren doesn't need to say it.

We've just **watched** Celeste take Charlie without interrogation and Sarah offer Lauren a door that locks.

The reader has eyes. 😄

I'd be tempted to end much smaller.

Perhaps she gets into the car.

Locks the doors.

Then Sarah messages:

> **Tell me when you're moving**

Lauren:

> **Moving**

And she drives.

Or your sprinkler/crows material could give us the ordinary world continuing around this enormous private rupture:

> I stepped outside and closed the door behind me.
>
> Somewhere down the street, a sprinkler hissed across somebody's lawn.
>
> Two crows argued on the power line.
>
> I put my bag on the passenger seat, locked the doors, and started the car.

**End.**

No peace yet.

No interpretation.

No solidarity thesis.

No romantic revelation.

Just Lauren in a locked car.

Going somewhere safe.

And Sarah is waiting there.

The reader who has just read *The Missing Spark* knows something Lauren doesn't.

**That's the spark.** ❤️

As for the title: **No Longer Needed** is doing the same explanatory work I'd remove from the prose. It tells us Lauren discovers she doesn't need Roger. I'd look for something concrete from the chapter instead. **A Door That Locks** immediately appeals to me — because it isn't about independence at all. It's about safety, Sarah's offer, and Lauren finally accepting help. *No Room* → *The Missing Spark* → **A Door That Locks** also has a rather lovely progression: nowhere to go → something newly recognised → somewhere safe to go.

I have a few issues with the story at the moment. Dot points:

* Lauren is a careful planner, a nurse who does nothing impulsively  
* *Yet*, finds herself ending a relationship *without* an exit strategy - very unlike Lauren  
* Particularly since it's not just her needing alternate accommodations, but Charlie too

**So, we need to develop a situation where Lauren has no choice but to leave - it is out of her control.** 

Potential scenarios

* Roger shows a rare violent side (don't want a moustache-twirler)
* Lauren is concerned for her safety for: reasons (may be justified or imagined)
* Roger gets drunk
* Something about the house makes it impossible to stay there

And then:

* while Lauren is dealing with the emotional trauma:  
* Sarah contacts Celeste;  
* they agree that having Charlie rent a room from Celeste can work from an economic and safely;  
* they both start developing a framework;  
* Lauren picks up Sarah;  
* Sarah shares the framework with Lauren on the way;  
* they head over to Celeste's;  
* and the meeting happens
