# Edit Workspace

Mara doesn’t test people the way teachers do.

Teachers announce the test, watch you sweat, and then moralise the result. Mara tests you the way you test a seam: under her fingers, quietly, looking for strain.

Charlie was back for a second morning. Same early arrival. Same clean hands. Same careful stillness. 

Mara didn’t say hello, but pointed at a tin on the table.

“Pins,” she said. “Sort them. Then you’re on the mending pile.”

Charlie nodded and poured the pins out onto a cloth. With a kind of tidy focus that made the task look dignified, he and began arranging them by length.

Wardrobe had its own rhythm — calm on the surface, precise underneath. Charlie had already started matching it — he had joined a system without trying to dominate it.

Mara’s next marker came ten minutes later, when she “accidentally” left a pair of vintage shears too close to the edge of the table. 

Charlie noticed the shears. His eyes flicked to it, then to Mara. He didn’t touch the shears. He nudged the cloth closer, stabilised the table edge with his palm, and slid the shears back with two fingers — careful, respectful — like he was returning a bird to its perch.

Mara didn’t look up. 

In fact, I didn't think she'd noticed at all. But presently, the smallest change in her mouth betrayed her: she knew. 

Of course she knew.

Ten minutes later, she called across the room.

“Rossignol. Bring me the blue painter’s tape.”

Charlie paused, his gaze shifting to the shelves. There were three blue tapes, different widths. He didn’t guess. He looked once at Mara, then asked quietly:

“Which width, Mara?”

Mara’s eyes lifted.

“Quarter-inch.”

Charlie retrieved the roll and brought it to her.

Charlie’s attention to Mara was constant and respectful.

Charlie’s attention to the room was caution. 

Charlie’s attention to me was— different.

It was as if his eyes kept finding me on their own, the way a compass needle finds north, and each time he realised he'd done it, he would look away with a start, like it was an invasion of privacy.

It was almost endearing.

Almost.

Mara sent him to the mending pile: a basket of small catastrophes — popped seams, torn cuffs, fraying apron ties: the difficult ones, the ones where haste would show. Charlie sat, studying each item the way he’d assessed the torn dress yesterday: calm, quiet. He chose thread that matched with an uncanny colour instinct. He stitched with even tension, no puckering, no desperate pulling.

Half an hour later, Mara handed Charlie a garment bag. 

“Hang that.”

It was heavier than it looked — wool, boning, metal closures. Charlie took it with both hands. He carried it the way you carried a treasured item, and when he reached the rail he stopped and studied spaces. Then he hung it in a place that made sense, not in the first empty gap.

Mara watched him out of the corner of her eye.

I stepped closer to Charlie’s table — it was time to introduce the next lesson.

“You’re stitching like you’ve done this for years.”

Charlie’s hands paused for half a second, needle hovering. Then he kept going.

“My mum,” he said, voice low. “She made it look fun. It was like a game — she gave me harder and harder things to fix. I had to sew them really neat and tidy. If you make it neat, people pay.”

“That’s true,” I replied. “And if you make it neat here, Mara doesn’t kill you.”

His mouth twitched into a fragile smile, one that quickly vanished. His eyes flick up to my face, and then away again, like a cat walking on a hot surface.

“Thanks,” he said.

“For what?”

“For— yesterday.” He looked down at his hands. “For— for getting me in.”

*I don’t want gratitude*.

I shook my head.

“*You* got you in,” I said. “You turned up. You worked.”

His shoulders loosened slightly. He threaded another needle, hands steady. Then, without looking at me, he murmured,

“I just—"

He stopped. 

I did what I always did: I decided what it meant.

“You mean you don’t want to make me uncomfortable.”

His head snapped up. Hazel eyes, startled. Then he looked down again, quick as shame.

“Yes,” he whispered.

“Fine. Let's keep it that way.”

He nodded hard, like he’d been given a rule. Then he added,

“It’s just— you’re— you’re a lot.”

I blinked.

“A lot?”

He winced. “I meant, you’re—" He paused. Thought. Finally: "You make things make sense.”

I let his words sit between us. His eyes flicked to mine after a bit, confused. Searching.

“Look, you’ve been living in rooms where you can’t win,” I told him. "Wardrobe is a room where you can. If you have strong feelings, aim them at your work. That will keep you safe.”

He stared at me. Then he nodded. Slowly.

“Okay.”

I turned to go back to the rail.

“I feel I should tell you, tell Mara now,“ he said, his words spilling out, rushed. ”Maintenance was just a summer job. It seems like everyone is investing in me here, and so I should tell you this, or it wouldn’t be fair to any of you.”

I paused, hand resting on a garment bag.

“Tell me what?”

He kept his eyes on the seam. Not me.

“I’m meant to be starting an apprenticeship next year. Welding. At the shipyard.” He swallowed. “Dad’s lined it up.”

That landed like a duck landing on ice but expecting water.

I looked at the way he held the needle. His arms. His long hair, tied in an elastic at the back of his head.

*Welder*.

*Make it make sense.*

“Did he line up maintenance for you as well?” I asked.

He shrugged, small. “Yeah. He wanted me to sort-of get ready for it. Get stronger. Learn tools. Be— useful.”

I sat down next to him slowly. I closed my eyes to concentrate for a moment, then looked at him properly.

“Let me get this straight.“ He was staring at nothing in front of him, biting the inside of his mouth. “You crashed and burned in maintenance.” I said. “And yet you think you have a career as boilermaker? How do you connect the dots?“

He flinched. “I don't know. I should try, though.”

“But you *did* try, Charlie. You held a ladder and lifted a toolbox and Graham, who isn't known for having a soft heart, did the right thing to let you go. And yet, you still want to become a boilermaker.” I shook my head. “Why?”

His jaw tightened. “What do you mean, why? I should have a job.”

I let my gaze drop deliberately — his hands, the way he held the fabric.

“Because that isn't how you work,” I said. “It's just *not*, Charlie.“

He frowned. “What do you mean?”

“You're not built for a job that involves force. You don't do force. Your approach to solving things is refined. Intelligent.” I gestured lightly toward the dress. “You just spent ten minutes undoing a mistake without punishing the fabric for it. You think those are required skills for a boilermaker?”

He didn’t answer.

“Your instructors will tell you to push harder,” I continued, voice even. “To move faster. To stop thinking and just do. And you’ll try. Because that’s what you’ve been told men are meant to do.”

His eyes flicked up to mine.

“I’m not saying it’s bad work,” I added, “for some men. I’m just saying it’s the wrong language for you.”

“It’s— solid work," he said, his voice a monotone. "Pays properly.”

“Yes, it does.” I nodded. “That work is ideal for people who can do it without breaking.”

He stared at the space in front of him, lips pursed.

“You think I can’t?”

He looked at me suddenly, almost accusingly. I held his gaze. Didn’t soften it.

“I think you’ll force yourself to try,” I said.

Silence stretched. The steamer hissed somewhere behind us.

“I just—" he began, then stopped. His voice dropped. “I just need solid work.”

There it was.

Survival.

I placed my finger lightly on the fabric near his seam.

“This is solid work,” I said.

He shook his head with a faint puff. “This is— dresses.”

“Mara’s work is engineering,” I said, flat.

His mouth went sideways.

“You were told to do maintenance work to become something you’re not,” I continued. “And then, you came here and were immediately able to do something useful because of who you already are.”

He didn’t look up.

“That’s not coincidence,” I said. "You don’t get to ignore that just because someone else had a plan for you."

His fingers tightened on the fabric.

“What if I continue on here,” he said slowly, “and then find out it wasn’t going to work?”

“Well, then you leave,” I replied. “But you’ll leave knowing you had a go.”

“Think about it, Charlie. Realistically. Let's say you go weld ships,” I said. “You’ll spend the first year trying not to be noticed. The second year, you'll try not to fail. The third year you'll spend convincing yourself it was the right choice. And given your track record at maintenance...” I stopped.

I could see that landed. Hard.

Across the room, Mara’s voice cut through the hiss of the steamer.

“Rossignol!”

Charlie’s head snapped up. “Yes Mara?”

Mara held up a sleeve with a tear near the cuff. “This one. Show us how to do it properly.”

Charlie went very still.

“Yes, Mara.”

He rose, took the sleeve, and walked to Mara’s table with careful confidence.

As he passed me, his eyes flicked to my face.

Being chosen is not an accident.

It’s a responsibility.

And I wasn’t going to let him waste it.
