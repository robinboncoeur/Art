Charlie doesn't merely lack the confidence to claim womanhood. He lacks the sense of entitlement to claim anything for himself.

And that changes the nature of his journey rather profoundly.

We've been approaching the question as though Charlie's difficulty is primarily epistemological: How does he discover what he is? How does he distinguish wanting to escape maleness from wanting to become female?

Those questions remain important.

But you've introduced another, perhaps more fundamental obstacle.

Even if Charlie discovers what he wants, what makes him think he's entitled to want it?

Consider the Charlie we've written.

He doesn't demand a place in Wardrobe. He accepts the work he's offered and makes himself useful. He doesn't insist that Mara recognise his intelligence; he demonstrates it through his work. When Celeste offers him friendship, he doesn't presume upon it. When people show him kindness, he's almost embarrassingly grateful.

“I can learn, if someone shows me how.”

We've always understood that as evidence of his openness to learning. But there's another dimension to it.

He's asking for instruction, not authority.

He's effectively saying: Tell me what's required of me, and I'll try to become someone who can do it.

You described not feeling you deserved the social status of woman.

I hear something different from merely not identifying as one. I hear a distinction between an identity that might feel deeply desirable and a social recognition you hesitate to ask others to grant.

I'm not going to tell you what your identity is. That belongs to you.

And that, I think, could be an extraordinarily powerful expression of her authority: a strong young woman deliberately declining to exercise power over someone who would willingly surrender it to her.

There's a sentence forming in my mind that might become a useful thematic touchstone for this part of Unhidden:

Charlie knows how to earn his place in someone else's world. He doesn't yet know that he's allowed to have a world of his own.

And perhaps that is why this part of the book has been so difficult to write.

We have been trying to discover what Charlie will choose.

But we may first need to show him learning that he is allowed to choose at all.

That's a much deeper story, my friend. And I think you've just opened another door into it. ❤️

## 5. Anger, testosterone, and the fear of becoming unsafe

## 6. The thread connecting all of this

I think there is a common pattern emerging, but it isn't necessarily gender.

It's Charlie's habit of evaluating himself against standards he believes he has failed to meet.

At school, academic achievement.

In the orchestra, musical accomplishment.

Among boys, masculinity.

Among women, perhaps an idealised standard of consideration, restraint and moral worth.

He enters Wardrobe and, for the first time, finds standards he can meet through abilities he already possesses.

That is liberating.

He can accept Mara's judgement that he's a competent worker because she can demonstrate it.

No report he can write.

That's a significant distinction.

And there's one final thought I'd like to leave with you tonight.

Why did you originally need Celeste to convince Charlie that he was a girl?

And I don't think the original premise was nearly as naïve as you're now inclined to regard it. The mechanism was naïve, perhaps. The underlying emotional need may have been considerably more profound.

Let me propose something.

Perhaps you wrote Celeste to give Charlie the permission you couldn't give yourself.

Not necessarily consciously. Indeed, I suspect that if this interpretation has merit, much of it was happening beneath the surface.

Consider the original arrangement.

Charlie is diffident. He doesn't claim things. He doesn't presume that his wishes should determine other people's behaviour. He is deeply uncomfortable with masculinity, yet cannot bring himself to claim womanhood.

And Celeste?

Celeste is everything he isn't in this particular respect.

She's confident. She's decisive. She possesses an unquestioned place among women. She has the authority, the social understanding, and the emotional certainty that Charlie lacks.

And, crucially, she is a woman.

What if the original story was an imaginative solution to an apparently insoluble personal problem?

Charlie cannot say, I am a girl.

And because the declaration comes from someone Charlie admires, trusts and recognises as belonging unquestionably to the category he cannot bring himself to claim, it carries an authority his own wishes do not.

He doesn't have to make the claim.

She makes it for him.

### But I think there's another layer

The original story allowed Charlie to arrive somewhere desirable without having to bear the full responsibility of choosing the journey.

### And look what happened when you rewrote it

In Unhidden, we've systematically taken away the mechanisms that allowed Charlie to avoid choosing.

Mara doesn't dress him to feminise him. She employs his abilities.

It has to be his own.

You've removed the device that made the original story emotionally comfortable.

The woman who could decide for him.
