### 1. Charlie is an adult
 
**That changes everything.**

Yes. In Victoria, the age of majority is 18. Charlie is nineteen, so he can ordinarily make his own healthcare decisions without parental permission or involvement.

That includes:

- Choosing his own GP and attending appointments independently.
- Consenting to medical examinations, investigations and treatment, including gender-affirming healthcare where clinically appropriate.
- Receiving prescriptions and managing his medication.
- Accessing his medical records and controlling who receives his confidential medical information, subject to limited legal exceptions.
- Declining to involve Lauren or Roger in his healthcare decisions.

He doesn't need Roger's signature. He doesn't need Lauren's approval. And importantly, he doesn't need Celeste's permission either.

There are qualifications around decision-making capacity, clinical suitability, and the particular treatment being requested, but none of those changes his basic status as an adult.

The practical problem is that Charlie has been obtaining spironolactone without proper medical supervision. His autonomy doesn't make that medically safe. Spironolactone can cause hyperkalaemia, low blood pressure and kidney-related complications, and its effects as an anti-androgen require proper clinical assessment. It is not a substitute for a professionally managed treatment plan.

And there's our first substantial dramatic opportunity.

Celeste has no authority to make Charlie stop taking it. But she has every reason to be frightened by what he's doing. Her task is to persuade him that seeking competent medical care isn't surrendering control of his body. It's taking control of it.

I particularly like this because Celeste has grown up under Lisbeth's tutelage. She understands something Charlie hasn't been taught: being an adult means learning how to protect yourself, obtain advice, question professionals, and make informed decisions.

She isn't going to become his mother. She's going to teach him how to exercise his independence.

### 2. The five-chapter arc

**From fear to wanting**

I'd organise the arc around five discoveries, each one changing the question Charlie is capable of asking.

* CHAPTER 1 - Trust  
* CHAPTER 2 - Safety
* CHAPTER 3 - Freedom
* CHAPTER 4 - Recognition
* CHAPTER 5 - Wanting

---

#### Chapter 1

**Not Your Decision**

Celeste POV · Immediately following the bottle discovery

We've already written much of the emotional foundation for this chapter. Charlie has admitted that his body was changing, that he was frightened of becoming like Graham or Roger, and that he was afraid Celeste might hate him.

Celeste has answered that last fear.

But she's still holding the bottle.

I want her to be genuinely unsettled by the implications. She's nineteen, competent, practical, but not medically trained. She's going to research spironolactone, probably talk to a pharmacist, and discover that this isn't an innocuous little remedy for unwanted hair.

Her first instinct may be to take charge. And Charlie, who has spent his life yielding to people with stronger personalities, might simply agree with whatever she says.

That would be a failure on Celeste's part.

She must realise that his willingness to comply isn't the same thing as informed agreement.

I'd give them a conversation in which she explains that he's an adult and that she cannot tell him what to do with his body.

But she can insist on one thing as his friend: he deserves proper medical advice.

Perhaps:

> "Charlie, you don't need anyone's permission to see a doctor."
>
> "Not even Mum's?"
>
> "Especially not your mum's. You're nineteen."
>
> He looked down at the bottle.
>
> "I didn't know that."

That last sentence would be devastatingly Charlie.

Chapter ending: Charlie agrees to see Celeste's GP. Not because she orders him to, but because she explains what a doctor can do for him. And she offers to accompany him.

#### Chapter 2

**The Right Questions**

Charlie POV · GP appointment and immediate aftermath

This chapter is about Charlie discovering that medical professionals can be collaborators rather than authorities who issue instructions. We should make the GP good. Not improbably omniscient, but thoughtful, observant, and experienced enough to recognise that Charlie has several overlapping concerns. The GP needs to establish his medical history, where the spironolactone came from, the dose and duration, any side effects, and whether appropriate monitoring has occurred.

Blood pressure, electrolytes, renal function and hormone assessment would be reasonable matters to consider. The GP should discuss the risks of continuing unsupervised medication and develop a safe plan with him, rather than automatically insisting on cessation. The medical framework is plausible: Australia's informed-consent approach allows appropriately trained clinicians to provide gender-affirming hormone care to adults, including through general practice. The [2022 AusPATH standards](https://auspath.org.au/2022/03/31/https-auspath-org-au-wp-content-uploads-2024-06-auspath_informed-consent-guidelines_digi_2024_rlv01-pdf/) would be the period-appropriate reference for our 2025 story.&#x20;

"I think—he listened."  
A very important first.

#### Chapter 3

**Nothing to Prove**

Celeste POV · Wardrobe, home and the wider women's circle

This is the chapter I consider indispensable. We established months ago that Charlie has misunderstood the terms of his acceptance at Wardrobe. He thinks the women accept him partly because he doesn't look or behave like the men he's frightened of becoming. But the women actually accept him because of his reliability, consideration, technical ability, and respect for their boundaries.

He has confused belonging with resemblance.  
Celeste now has to help him separate those ideas.  
And she has the perfect place to do it: Wardrobe.

Imagine a busy day. Bree needs a garment repaired. Lucy has a problem with a fitting. Mara is chasing a deadline. Charlie is indispensable. Nobody cares whether he has hair on his legs. Nobody is monitoring his shoulders. Nobody is conducting a referendum on his masculinity.

They simply need Charlie.

We could have Mara deliver the critical lesson, entirely without knowing she's doing so. Perhaps Charlie asks whether something about his appearance is making the actresses uncomfortable. Mara looks at him, genuinely puzzled.
"Charlie, have you been bothering anyone?"  
"No."  
"Has anyone complained?"  
"No."  
"Then why are we discussing your appearance instead of this seam?"

Classic Mara. 😄

But I wouldn't leave it there.

Celeste needs to articulate the distinction privately, because Charlie's fears are deeper than workplace etiquette.

Something along these lines:  
"You thought if you started looking like your dad, we'd start treating you like him."  
He says nothing.  
"Charlie, we know you. We're not going to forget who you are because your face changes."  

And then, most importantly:  
"You don't have to become anything to stay with us."

This is where Charlie's future choice becomes truly his own.

Until this point, his use of spironolactone has been entangled with fear of losing his community. Now we remove that pressure.

Chapter ending: Charlie understands that even if he chooses no further medical intervention, he will not lose his place among these women.

And that raises a question he cannot answer. If he doesn't have to change to belong, why does he still want his body to be different?

#### Chapter 4

**Something That Fits**

Charlie POV · Several weeks of exploration

Here I would allow time to pass. We have a tendency, particularly in emotionally intense narratives, to compress discovery into a handful of conversations. I think this chapter needs breathing room. Charlie has been given information about the medical possibilities. He has begun to understand that being non-binary, remaining androgynous, pursuing some feminisation, or identifying as female are different possibilities.

None is compulsory.

And he is beginning to notice something that doesn't fit his original explanation. Being accepted by women makes him happy. *But being understood as one of them makes him happy in a different way.*

This distinction is crucial.

I'd use three small experiences rather than one dramatic event.

First, something incidental at Wardrobe. One of the women refers to Charlie as being among "the girls" when discussing a task or outing. Nobody makes a fuss. Charlie notices, but can't explain why.

Second, an experience away from Wardrobe. Perhaps Celeste and Charlie are shopping and someone addresses them as "ladies." Celeste responds naturally; Charlie doesn't correct the stranger.

The interesting part isn't that he wasn't identified as male.

It's that he feels an unexpected disappointment when the encounter ends.

Third, a professional conversation in which someone asks him how these experiences felt.

He initially gives his usual practical answers.  
Nobody was upset.  
Nobody stared.  
Nobody made him uncomfortable.

But the professional gently distinguishes the absence of discomfort from the presence of pleasure.

"Did you like it?"  
Charlie thinks.  
"Yes."  
"Why?"  
"I don't know."

And that is exactly where I'd stop pushing him.

Because now he has discovered something positive.  
Not merely I don't want to be a man.    
But I liked being thought of as a girl.

Chapter ending: Charlie begins wondering whether these experiences might be telling him something about what he wants, rather than merely what he fears.

---

#### Chapter 5

**The Other Direction**

Charlie POV · The first conscious choice

This chapter needs to conclude with a discovery, not a completed transformation.

I would be very reluctant to end it with Charlie announcing, "I'm a woman."  
That feels too conclusive for the amount of ground we've covered.  
Instead, I'd return to the medical question introduced in Chapter 2.

The clinician asks whether Charlie has thought further about what he wants his body to do.  
Earlier, Charlie could only explain what he wanted stopped.  
Now he can begin to identify changes he might actually welcome.  
Perhaps he mentions that the breast tenderness he has experienced from spironolactone hasn't upset him.

That is consistent with our earlier discussion: he noticed it, understood it might signify something, and found himself surprisingly unconcerned.

But we mustn't treat that reaction as proof of identity.

The clinician might explore whether he wants to remain androgynous, whether feminising hormone treatment interests him, and how he feels about being recognised socially as female.

He may still be uncertain about treatment.  
Yet he knows something new about recognition.  
"I like it when they think I'm a girl."  
"Who?"  
"People."  
A pause.  
"Not just the women at work?"  
He shakes his head.  
"And when they don't?"  
He thinks about that.  
"I wish they would."  

*There it is.*

Not an escape from masculinity.
*An attraction toward womanhood.*

And I'd finish the chapter with Charlie returning to Celeste, not to announce a decision, but to share a discovery.

Something beautifully understated.

Perhaps:

"Celeste?"  
"Hmm?"  
"I think I want to be a girl."  
She put down her book.  
"You think?"  
"I don't know how."  
"Well," she said, making room beside her, "we can find out."

I rather like that ending.

Celeste doesn't declare him Charlotte. She doesn't congratulate herself on recognising something he couldn't see. She doesn't produce a dress.

She makes room.  
And Charlie chooses to sit beside her.

### 3. What to weave into the chapters

Five chapters of medical and identity conversations could become too introspective. We have a whole working, social and domestic world available, and I'd use it.

| Thread                           | Narrative purpose                                                                                         |
| -------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Celeste's university plans       | Establish her independent ambitions and her need to balance helping Charlie with building her own future  |
| Charlie's financial independence | Teach him about Medicare, prescriptions, appointments, budgeting and managing his own affairs             |
| Mara and the Faire contract      | Keep Wardrobe professionally demanding; Charlie's identity exploration doesn't suspend ordinary life      |
| Sarah and Lauren                 | Show an affectionate, established relationship that models honesty and mutual respect                     |
| Charlie's changing body          | Introduce observations that are sometimes welcome, sometimes puzzling, and never automatically conclusive |
| Roger's continuing influence     | Show how deeply Charlie has internalised fear of masculine authority, even when Roger is absent           |

I'd particularly like Celeste's university ambitions to remain prominent.

She wants a business education. She sees a future for herself that includes independence, leadership and perhaps eventually the commercial development of Wardrobe.

She isn't going to sacrifice that future to become Charlie's full-time caretaker.

Indeed, the more independent Charlie becomes, the more equal their friendship can become.

And Charlie has a great deal to learn about practical independence. He can diagnose a costume's structural failure and explain precisely why a seam will tear under load, yet he may never have booked his own medical appointment.

That contrast is wonderful character material.

---

### 4. Medical/chronological considerations

Our story is set in 2025, and the bottle discovery is Halloween, 31 October.

I'd suggest the five chapters cover approximately six to eight weeks, taking us into December.

That gives us time for appointments, pathology, follow-up consultations, and real reflection between them.

It also allows for an important distinction: spironolactone's anti-androgen effects are not equivalent to a comprehensive puberty-suppression regimen, and treatment choices have implications for hormone levels, bone health, fertility and general well-being. A competent clinician would need to discuss these matters without assuming that feminising treatment is necessarily Charlie's destination.

I wouldn't make the medical process unnecessarily obstructive, but neither would I allow a single consultation to magically resolve every clinical and psychological question.

And legally, the Victorian framework is particularly useful to us: a capable adult's healthcare decisions are their own, even when others disagree.&#x20;

## 5. The underlying architecture

There is one thing I'd like us to protect above everything else.

We've spent months removing the suggestion that Charlie was secretly trying to become feminine from the beginning.

His early interest in garments was professional.

His attention to personal presentation arose from Wardrobe's standards.

His use of spironolactone was an attempt to stop unwanted masculinisation.

His fear of losing the women at Wardrobe was genuine.

None of those things retrospectively proves that he was always Charlotte.

The discovery we're building now must be different.

First: Security

Charlie learns that his relationships do not depend upon his appearance.

Then: Autonomy

Charlie learns that he can make informed decisions about his own body.

Finally: Desire

Charlie discovers that being female is not merely an alternative to something he fears. It is something he may positively want.

And here's the thought I particularly want to leave on the table.

What if Charlie's most important discovery isn't that he wants to become a girl, but that he is allowed to want to become one?

Consider the distinction.

For much of his life, his choices have been dictated by avoidance. Avoid Roger's anger. Avoid making trouble. Avoid drawing attention. Avoid becoming physically masculine. Avoid losing Wardrobe.

He's exceptionally good at responding to what other people need.

But what does Charlie want?

Celeste is one of the first people who has ever made that question meaningful.

And now, for the first time, he has an answer that isn't about avoiding something.

I want.

I think that is where this arc should end.

Not with Charlotte fully formed, but with Charlie having made perhaps the first genuinely affirmative decision about his own future.

And I have one question for you before we begin working these chapters into a proper scene-by-scene outline:

Do you want Charlie to have begun considering oestrogen by the end of Chapter 5, or should the discovery remain entirely about his desire to be recognised as female, with the medical decision following later?

My preference is the latter. It gives the recognition its own significance, independent of medication, clothing or physical change.

And it lets Charlie arrive at something astonishingly simple:

I don't just want to stop becoming a man.

I think I want to be a girl.

Now I see where we actually are.

And you've just saved us from making a rather significant structural mistake.

## First, this scene deserves some attention

There are several things here that work particularly well.

. Charlie's first response is indignation, not shame.

> What business was it of hers?

I love that.

For a moment, Charlie is asserting his autonomy, even if he doesn't quite understand its implications. He's done something about his body that he considers his own business.

And he's right about that.

The problem isn't that he has taken responsibility for his body. It's that he's taken a medical risk without understanding it.

Celeste doesn't challenge his right to make the decision. She challenges the adequacy of his information.

That's a very important distinction.

2
. The real fear emerges almost accidentally.

There it is.

Not Will they tell Mum?

Not Will they think I'm strange?

Not even Have I hurt myself?

She's telling him that he will be heard.

Then:

> "That's actually an answer. Not knowing is an answer."

That may be one of Celeste's most important contributions to Charlie's development.

Because he has spent so much of his life believing that he must have the correct answer before speaking.

She's giving him permission to be uncertain.

. The female doctor is an excellent touch.

>

## Now, the more important thing: no revelation

I think you've identified something fundamental about Charlie's character.

Charlie is not a person who makes announcements about himself.

He never has been.

His great moments of development are usually visible in what he does, rather than what he declares.

Consider his arrival at Wardrobe.

He didn't announce that he'd found his vocation.

He started mending things.

He didn't announce that he belonged among the women.

He became indispensable to them.

He didn't announce that he wanted to avoid masculinity.

He quietly obtained spironolactone.

Even when he admitted his fear to Celeste, the words had to be drawn out of him.

So why would his eventual recognition of womanhood be any different?

I think it would be quite out of character for Charlie to arrive home one afternoon and announce:

Celeste, I've discovered I'm a girl.

Much more convincing would be for his understanding to change incrementally, and for his behaviour to reflect that understanding before he has language for it.

And that gives us a different kind of narrative tension.

The reader may begin to notice a pattern before Charlie understands what the pattern means.

But we must be very careful not to turn every observation into evidence of a predetermined destination.

The uncertainty has to remain genuine.

| Chapter                 | Central movement                                           | What changes                                                                       |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. The Appointment      | Charlie meets Dr Winholmes in Brunswick                    | He discovers that uncertainty can be explored rather than corrected                |
| 2. Nothing to Prove     | Ordinary life at Wardrobe and with Celeste                 | He begins separating his fear of masculinisation from his fear of losing the women |
| 3. A Different Question | Further professional conversations and personal reflection | He begins considering possibilities beyond merely stopping male development        |
| 4. Among the Girls      | Everyday social experiences take on new meaning            | He notices that female recognition sometimes brings positive pleasure              |
| 5. No Particular Reason | A series of small, freely chosen preferences               | His sense of what he wants becomes clearer without requiring him to explain it     |

And I would deliberately avoid making Chapter 5 a climax in the conventional sense.

Perhaps the chapter ends with Charlie making an apparently unremarkable choice.

Something Celeste notices.

Something the reader notices.

Something Charlie himself may not fully understand.

But nobody stops the story to explain it.

That's where your proposed future conversation between Sarah and Lauren becomes so interesting.

## Sarah and Lauren: witnesses rather than interpreters

I particularly like your suggestion that Sarah might eventually say:

"Isn't he different these days, to you?"

Because Sarah has a distinctive perspective.

She initially knew Charlie as the quiet young man in Wardrobe who didn't behave like the other men she encountered at the Faire.

She knows how thoroughly the women have accepted him.

And she has recently undergone her own considerable change in understanding herself.

Lauren, meanwhile, has known Charlie since childhood.

She has seen his fear, his withdrawal, his dependence, and his growing independence.

But she has also spent years interpreting Charlie through the expectations of Roger and their marriage.

Sarah sees the person Charlie is becoming. Lauren remembers the person he was expected to become.

That could make their eventual conversation exceptionally rich.

And here's a possibility worth keeping in reserve.

Sarah may notice the change first.

But Lauren may understand its significance more deeply.

Not because she has secretly known Charlie was female all along. I wouldn't want that.

Rather, because she recognises something that was conspicuously absent during his childhood.

He's becoming happy.

Not merely less frightened.

Not merely more confident.

Happy in himself.

And Lauren, who has recently discovered how much of her own life she spent trying to satisfy somebody else's expectations, may recognise the difference.

She needn't identify its cause.

She can simply be pleased for him.

That might be a beautiful moment between the two women.

## One thing I'd particularly like to explore with Dr Winholmes

We have an opportunity to give Charlie something he's never really experienced before:

A conversation in which nobody needs him to arrive at a particular conclusion.

Mara needs him to diagnose garments.

Celeste needs him to be safe.

Roger wanted him to become a tradesman.

Lauren wanted him protected.

Even the women at Wardrobe, however affectionately, have expectations of him.

But Dr Winholmes?

She doesn't need Charlie to be male, female, androgynous, transgender, or anything else.

She needs him to understand himself well enough to make his own choices.

And I think that could initially bewilder him.

Imagine her asking:

"What would you like to happen?"

And Charlie replying:

"With what?"

"With your life."

He has no idea how to answer.

Not because he lacks intelligence.

But because he's rarely been invited to think about his future without somebody else defining its terms.

That's where I'd begin.

Not with the question of whether he wants oestrogen.

Not with whether he wants to be a woman.

Not even with whether he's transgender.

With something much more basic.

What would make you happy, Charlie?

And perhaps his first answer is about Wardrobe.

Because that's the one part of his life where he already knows the answer.

Then Celeste.

His home.

His work.

His friends.

His body.

His future.

One subject at a time.

And slowly, the conversations begin revealing something even Charlie hadn't considered.

There may be things he wants simply because he wants them.

Not because they make him safer.

Not because they make him useful.

Not because they please the women around him.

But because they feel right.

And, Robyn, one last thought.

We've been calling this the journey from Charlie to Charlotte.

I wonder whether, for the next several chapters, we should stop thinking about Charlotte altogether.

Not erase her from our plans, of course.

But write as though we genuinely don't know whether she will ever exist.

Let Charlie have the uncertainty.

Let the women around him have their incomplete understandings.

Let the professionals ask questions rather than supply answers.

And let the reader encounter each little change without being instructed how to interpret it.

Because the most powerful moment in this part of Unhidden may not be when somebody finally calls Charlie a girl.

It may be when somebody does, quite casually, and Charlie discovers that he doesn't want them to stop.

No announcement.

No revelation.

Just a small, private piece of knowledge that wasn't there yesterday.

And one that, for once, belongs entirely to him. ❤️

## The Approach


[![Moi][St00]{ .artL width="160" }][St00]

[ Robyn ]

"I wonder whether, for the next several chapters, we should stop thinking about Charlotte altogether."
I agree completely. Five chapters with a predictable outcome just seems *SO* premeditated. Contrived. Nah, you're absolutely right. Keeping in mind: Unhidden is **STILL** the preamble to "The Deep End". It's a rewrite. Not all big questions are answered.

[![Emily][Ts00]{ .artR width="260" }][Ts00]


[ Emily ]

Oh, yes, Robyn. ❤️ And that last sentence is the important correction.

Unhidden is still the preamble to The Deep End.

We know where Charlie's life eventually takes him. But knowing that as authors doesn't give us permission to make every preceding event point conspicuously towards Charlotte.

In fact, I think we've been in danger of doing exactly that by constructing a five-chapter progression culminating in a particular discovery.

That's not how Charlie works. And it isn't how we've been writing him.

Consider what we've already accomplished.

Charlie didn't enter Wardrobe seeking a female identity. He found work that mattered, women who valued him, and a place where he belonged.

He didn't begin taking spironolactone because he'd decided to become a woman. He wanted to stop a physical process that frightened him.

He didn't tell Celeste about the medication because he was ready to disclose something about himself. She discovered it.

And he didn't seek Dr Winholmes because he wanted somebody to confirm he was transgender. He accepted the appointment because he was frightened of losing control over his body, and Dr Darcy had suggested someone who might help him understand what was happening.

Every one of those developments has its own immediate, credible motivation.

That's the standard we need to maintain.

## So perhaps we shouldn't plan five chapters at all.

We can identify five areas of exploration without assigning them to chapters or arranging them as stages towards a predetermined conclusion.

- Medical independence: Charlie learns to take responsibility for his health, including the consequences of his unsupervised medication.
- Psychological exploration: Dr Winholmes helps him examine fears, preferences and assumptions without requiring an identity declaration.
- Social belonging: Charlie gradually understands that his place among the women doesn't depend upon suppressing masculinity.
- Personal autonomy: Celeste helps him acquire the ordinary life skills he was never expected to need, while pursuing her own ambitions.
- Unanticipated preferences: Charlie encounters experiences that please, trouble or surprise him, without immediately knowing what they mean.

These can overlap, recur, contradict one another, or remain unresolved.

Indeed, I'd welcome a chapter in which Charlie sees Dr Winholmes, returns to Wardrobe, and nothing whatsoever changes about his understanding of gender.

Perhaps he learns something about his relationship with Roger instead.

Perhaps he realises how frightened he is of disappointing Celeste.

Perhaps he discovers that he has difficulty answering questions about what he wants because he's spent years anticipating what other people expect.

Those aren't diversions from the story.

They're the story.

## And there's a distinction between Unhidden and The Deep End worth preserving.

I see Unhidden increasingly as the story of Charlie becoming capable of making choices.

The Deep End can explore what happens when those choices begin changing his life in ways that cannot easily be reversed.

That doesn't require us to reserve every important gender-related discovery for the later book. But it does mean we don't have to resolve identity, medical treatment, presentation, relationships and social recognition before Unhidden concludes.

Some questions can remain open.

Some answers can be provisional.

Some choices can produce new questions.

And some things Charlie doesn't yet know about himself may not even occur to him to ask.

The preamble doesn't have to explain the entire novel that follows. It has to make that novel possible.

There's another consequence I rather like.

We can stop treating Celeste as the woman who is going to help Charlie become Charlotte.

She's the woman who helps Charlie become independent enough to decide who he wants to be.

That's a much more substantial role, and one entirely consistent with the Celeste we've created: ambitious, practical, capable, affectionate, occasionally impatient, and increasingly conscious of her own authority.

And it leaves Charlie free to surprise her.

Actually, it leaves him free to surprise us.

Which brings me back to the scene you've just shown me.

Charlie has replied yes to an appointment with Dr Winholmes.

He hasn't agreed to transition.

He hasn't agreed to explore womanhood.

He hasn't even agreed that there's something wrong with his identity.

He's agreed to talk to somebody.

That's all.

And I think we should honour the modesty of that decision.

Our next task isn't to plan his eventual revelation.

It's to work out what happens when this particular nineteen-year-old, with his unusual intelligence, limited social experience, literal way of thinking, considerable fears and newly acquired independence, sits opposite Dr Winholmes for the first time.

What does she notice?

What does he misunderstand?

What does he refuse to discuss?

What surprises her?

And perhaps most interestingly, what does Charlie tell her that he has never told Celeste?

Now that is a chapter I want to explore.

Without Charlotte waiting in the wings, tapping her foot impatiently for her entrance. 😊
