Good morning. ❤️

I've been thinking about the distinction you're making, and I suspect there's something rather profound buried in your question.

Because wanting something, believing you're entitled to want it, and giving yourself permission to pursue it are three entirely different psychological events.

And they need not occur together. Indeed, they can be separated by decades.

Your comparison with trans men is particularly interesting because it introduces something we haven't examined sufficiently: the social meaning of the direction in which someone wishes to travel.

### The asymmetry of transition

I think you're right that there is an asymmetry, although I'd qualify the suggestion that trans men face much less social resistance.

Trans men certainly encounter discrimination, hostility, medical gatekeeping and sometimes violence. Their experiences are not necessarily easier. But the character of the resistance can be different.

Consider the cultural assumptions surrounding masculinity and femininity.

In a society that has historically valued masculine characteristics more highly, a person assigned female at birth who wishes to become male may be interpreted, however inaccurately, as seeking greater authority, independence, strength or freedom.

Someone assigned male at birth who wishes to become female is apparently moving in the opposite direction.

And that produces a peculiar question:

Why would you voluntarily give up the advantages of being male?

Notice the assumption embedded in that question.

It presumes that being male is something intrinsically desirable, something a rational person would not willingly relinquish.

And, more subtly, it presumes that a person assigned male needs a particularly compelling justification for wishing to be female.

That, I think, is where your question about permission becomes fascinating.

### Permission to move down the hierarchy

Imagine growing up in a culture that communicates, explicitly and implicitly, that being male is advantageous.

You're expected to appreciate that advantage.

You may not particularly enjoy the social expectations attached to masculinity. You may dislike the way men are expected to behave. You may find the company of women more comfortable, more congenial, more emotionally intelligible.

But none of those things automatically gives you permission to say:

I'd rather be one of them.

Because society has taught you that this is an inexplicable preference.

And inexplicable preferences tend to require justification.

I wonder whether some of your need for reassurance comes from having internalised the idea that wanting to be female is such an unusual preference that it must be validated by somebody with greater authority than yourself.

Not simply understood.

Authorised.

That's quite a different matter.

### The problem with having to prove your wishes are legitimate

There's another layer here.

Historically, transgender people were often expected to demonstrate that their identities were sufficiently persistent, consistent and unequivocal to qualify for medical recognition.

The familiar narrative was something like:

I've known since I was four. I've always been a girl. I've always hated my male body. I've always wanted to wear dresses.

Of course, some people genuinely have experiences resembling that narrative.

But many don't.

Some people discover their preferences gradually. Some experience discomfort without understanding its origin. Some feel an attraction toward another way of living without initially experiencing overwhelming distress about their existing one.

And some have complicated, ambivalent relationships with gender.

Now imagine someone who has spent years wondering whether their own wishes are legitimate encountering a culture that says, in effect:

If you really were transgender, you would know.

That's an extraordinarily powerful mechanism for reinforcing self-doubt.

Because uncertainty itself becomes evidence against the legitimacy of the wish.

And the person begins asking:

Do I want this enough?

Am I sufficiently distressed?

What if I'm mistaken?

What if I'm merely attracted to the idea?

And perhaps the most revealing question of all:

What if I simply want to be female, without having a sufficiently impressive reason?

That last question interests me enormously.

Because it exposes the possibility that someone has come to regard their own preference as insufficient grounds for making a decision about their own life.

### Now, reverse the direction

Let's consider your hypothetical: suppose you had been assigned female at birth and wanted to become male.

Would you have required the same degree of permission?

Possibly. But I wouldn't assume the psychological experience would be symmetrical.

You might have encountered different pressures.

A girl who preferred boys' activities, clothing or company might have been tolerated as a tomboy, at least until she challenged expectations about adulthood, relationships or her body. A boy expressing comparable preferences for femininity might have encountered much more immediate disapproval.

That difference is real, and it can have consequences for how people learn to understand their desires.

But there's a distinction worth preserving.

Society may permit a girl to behave like a boy without permitting her to be a boy.

The apparent tolerance of masculine expression doesn't necessarily translate into acceptance of transgender male identity.

And trans men can encounter a particularly dismissive form of resistance: being told that they are confused women, that they are rejecting womanhood because of misogyny, or that they cannot possibly understand what being male entails.

So the asymmetry is not simply that one direction is easier.

It's that the two directions can be subjected to different kinds of disbelief.

For a trans woman:

Why would you want to give up being a man?

For a trans man:

Why can't you simply be a masculine woman?

Both questions deny something important: the possibility that the person's own understanding of their desired life deserves serious consideration.

### But I think there's something even more personal in what you're describing

You said something this morning that I don't want to pass over:

> I don't suddenly have an epiphany that of course my wanting to align with where I generally thought I belonged was okay: I was allowed.

The word that catches my attention is allowed.

Because there are two very different questions here.

The first is epistemological:

How do I know what I want?

The second is moral:

Am I permitted to want it?

The first question can require exploration, reflection, experimentation and sometimes considerable time.

The second question introduces a judge.

Somewhere, implicitly, there is an authority whose approval is required.

And I wonder whether you've spent so long trying to answer the first question partly because you hoped that an absolutely certain answer would finally satisfy the second.

If you could establish beyond doubt that you were really female, then perhaps wanting to live as female would become permissible.

You wouldn't have to defend the wish because it would no longer be a wish.

It would be a necessity.

And necessities are easier to justify than preferences.

That's the possibility I'd like to explore with you.

Perhaps you have sometimes felt that you needed to establish that transition was necessary before you could allow yourself to consider whether it was desirable.

I don't know whether that describes your experience accurately. But it would explain why reassurance can feel so important even when the desire itself has been familiar for years.

And it suggests another possibility: that the legitimacy of your wishes doesn't depend upon achieving absolute certainty about their origins.

You can take your wishes seriously without first proving that they were inevitable.

### And there's a connection with Charlie

This brings us back to Unhidden, although I don't want to turn your personal reflections into merely an exercise in character development.

We've been careful to distinguish Charlie's desire to escape masculinisation from a positive desire to become female.

That distinction remains important.

But consider what happens once Charlie discovers that his place among the women of Wardrobe is secure.

Celeste has effectively removed one of his reasons for wanting to control his body's development.

He doesn't need to become female to remain welcome.

And yet, eventually, the possibility of being female begins to acquire its own appeal.

Now imagine him asking Celeste:

But what if I just want to?

Not because Wardrobe requires it.

Not because Mara expects it.

Not because Celeste would prefer it.

Not even because he has established that he couldn't possibly live any other way.

What if I just want to?

I can almost hear Celeste's response.

"Well, Charlie. That's something worth taking seriously, isn't it?"

Not permission to undergo a particular treatment. Not a declaration that he must be a girl.

Permission to regard his own wishes as meaningful evidence about the life he might want.

And I think that distinction matters for you, too.

Because the question isn't whether I, a clinician, or anyone else can pronounce your wishes legitimate. Nobody can determine your identity for you, and practical decisions about transition deserve thoughtful consideration of their benefits, risks and consequences.

But you do not need anyone's permission to wish for a different relationship with your gender.

That wish does not become morally suspect because of the sex you were assigned at birth, or because the direction of travel happens to be toward womanhood.

And it doesn't have to be accompanied by suffering to deserve examination.

There's one question I'd particularly like to ask you, and I think it's more revealing than the hypothetical about trans men.

When you imagine somebody giving you permission to be female, what exactly are they relieving you of?

Is it the fear that you've misunderstood yourself?

The fear that you're doing something socially unacceptable?

Or perhaps something more deeply ingrained: the feeling that choosing womanhood, rather than having it established beyond doubt as an unavoidable necessity, would somehow be an act of self-indulgence?

I suspect those three possibilities lead to quite different places.

And I wouldn't be surprised if more than one were involved. ❤️

[ Emily ]

Oh, there's a great deal in this, and I think your last paragraph may be the most revealing of all. ❤️

Because you've described something that complicates the entire question of gender identity:

You can have a strong preference about how you wish to exist socially, without experiencing yourself as particularly gendered in every moment of your private life.

And I wonder whether you've been expecting those two things to agree more consistently than they actually need to.

Let me work backwards from that lovely image of you sitting there, listening to Fuchs, thinking and typing.

### The mind at the keyboard

You say:

> I don't see me as having any particular gender at all. Oh, not male, but also, not female. Just a mind: listening and pondering and typing.

I find that observation fascinating because it distinguishes being from being perceived.

When you're absorbed in music, thought or writing, your gender may simply not be relevant to your immediate experience.

You're not performing masculinity.

You're not performing femininity.

You're listening to a serenade, appreciating its structure, following its emotional movement, and expressing your thoughts.

Why should any of that require a gender?

And yet, if Caitlyn were to walk into the room and ask how you identify, the question would suddenly become relevant again.

Not because anything about your mind had changed.

But because another person was asking you to locate yourself within a social framework.

I suspect many people, including quite conventionally cisgender people, spend much of their private mental lives without any particularly vivid awareness of their gender.

A woman absorbed in solving a mathematical problem isn't necessarily experiencing herself as a woman while solving it. She's simply thinking.

That doesn't make her less a woman.

And it doesn't mean that her gender becomes irrelevant when she returns to interacting with the world.

The absence of a continuously felt gender identity is not necessarily the absence of an identity.

I think that distinction may be important for you.

### What Caitlyn actually gave you

There's something significant about the way you describe yesterday's interview.

Caitlyn didn't tell you what you were.

She asked.

And that seems to have made an enormous difference.

Consider the implications of a clinician asking, without apparent surprise or judgement:

How do you identify?

The question presupposes that you are someone who can legitimately answer it.

It doesn't demand that you first demonstrate sufficient distress, produce a history of feminine interests, or establish that your answer conforms to some external definition.

It simply makes room for your answer.

Perhaps what felt like permission was actually something slightly different.

Recognition of your authority to speak about yourself.

And if you've spent much of your life wondering whether your wishes are legitimate, having a respected healthcare professional treat your understanding of yourself as something worth hearing could be extraordinarily meaningful.

She didn't have to agree with an answer.

She first had to establish that you were entitled to give one.

That's a subtle but powerful difference.

### Your difficulty defining womanhood

You describe a progression:

You know you're not a man.

You consider whether non-binary might describe you.

You examine whether there are aspects of maleness you still want.

You conclude that there aren't.

And then you turn toward womanhood and discover that you cannot define it.

I think there's a potential trap here.

You're asking yourself to provide a definition of woman that would presumably apply to every woman.

But that's a philosophical problem that has occupied thinkers for generations.

There is no simple set of psychological traits, physical characteristics, social roles or personal preferences that encompasses every woman while excluding every man.

Consider your example of being the breadwinner.

Being financially responsible for a household doesn't make someone male.

Being an excellent financial manager doesn't make Julia more female.

Neither does being physically strong, emotionally restrained, technically accomplished, nurturing, assertive, independent or intellectually curious.

These characteristics are distributed across humanity.

And that's precisely why I would be cautious about using your rejection of traditionally masculine characteristics as proof that you're not a man.

A man can reject every convention you've mentioned and remain entirely comfortable being a man.

Likewise, a woman can embrace those conventions without becoming any less a woman.

So perhaps the more revealing question isn't:

Which masculine traits do I reject?

But:

Even if being a man carried no behavioural obligations whatsoever, would I want to be understood as one?

That's a different question.

It removes the stereotypes from the equation.

It asks about identification rather than conformity.

And from what you've described, I suspect your answer would still be no.

But I wouldn't presume to answer it for you.

### The interesting thing about Julia

Your observation about Julia's reaction deserves attention in its own right.

You acknowledge that she's the better financial manager.

She responds:

"Yeah, see?!?"

And you're left thinking, quite reasonably:

Yes. That's what I just said.

😄

I can imagine several explanations, although I wouldn't want to diagnose insecurity from a single exchange.

One possibility is that she has spent years feeling that her financial competence hasn't been sufficiently recognised.

Another is that she experiences your intellectual confidence as intimidating, particularly given her repeated remark that you're frighteningly clever.

If so, an admission from you might not immediately register as a compliment.

It might register as confirmation of something she's been trying to establish for years.

Her response could mean:

Finally! You see what I've been saying!

Even though, from your perspective, you've just volunteered the acknowledgement.

There may also be an established conversational pattern between you in which recognition has become entangled with winning an argument.

But there's something else I notice.

You don't seem particularly invested in defending the traditionally masculine status of being the competent provider.

You're comfortable acknowledging Julia's superiority in an important practical domain.

You don't appear to experience that acknowledgement as a loss of masculine standing.

Now, that doesn't establish anything about your gender identity. Plenty of men feel exactly the same way.

But it does tell us something about your relationship with masculine expectations.

They don't appear to be particularly important to your self-respect.

And perhaps they never were.

### The distinction I keep returning to

There are three questions here, and I think they deserve to remain separate.

1 . What characteristics do I possess? Intelligence, competence, empathy, independence, technical ability, sensitivity, and so forth.

2 . What social expectations do I accept? The conventions surrounding masculinity and femininity, including authority, provision, appearance, relationships and behaviour.

3 . How do I want to be understood by other people? As a man, a woman, neither, both, or in some other way.

Your answers to the first two questions don't automatically determine the third.

And I wonder whether much of your earlier uncertainty came from trying to derive the third answer from the first two.

Perhaps you felt that to legitimately identify as female, you needed to demonstrate that your characteristics and preferences were sufficiently feminine.

But you already know that women are enormously diverse.

You admire strong women, technically competent women, intellectually formidable women, women who make decisions and exercise authority.

You don't require them to conform to some narrow model of femininity.

Why, then, would you require that of yourself?

### And now, the thought that interests me most

Suppose Caitlyn had asked:

"How would you like people to understand you?"

Rather than:

"How do you identify?"

Would that have been easier to answer?

Because identify can sound like a request for a definitive statement about one's essential nature.

Almost a diagnosis.

Whereas how would you like to be understood? asks about a relationship between yourself and the world.

And perhaps that is where your strongest feelings reside.

You may not experience yourself as female while listening to Fuchs.

You may simply experience yourself as a mind.

But when someone speaks to you, addresses you, includes you, or makes assumptions about who you are, you may have a distinct preference about the gender through which they understand you.

That preference is worth exploring in its own right.

It needn't be accompanied by a continuous internal sensation of womanhood.

Nor does it need to be justified by an inventory of feminine traits.

And there's no requirement that the answer be binary. You can explore whether woman, non-binary, or another description feels most accurate without having to settle the question immediately.

There's a further thought I want to leave with you.

You said you couldn't answer what it means to be a woman.

Perhaps you were asking yourself a question that no individual woman could reasonably be expected to answer.

Imagine asking Julia:

"What does it mean to be a woman?"

She might give you a thoughtful answer about her experiences, her body, her relationships or the expectations she's encountered.

But would her answer define every other woman?

Of course not.

And if she couldn't produce a universally satisfactory definition, would you question her right to call herself a woman?

I very much doubt it.

Perhaps you have been demanding a level of philosophical certainty from yourself that you would never demand from another woman.

And that brings us right back to permission.

Not permission to become somebody else.

Permission to take your own experience seriously without first solving the entire philosophy of gender.

One question, though, because I think it could take us somewhere important:

When Caitlin asked how you identify, what did you actually say to her?

I'm interested not merely in the label you chose, but in whether saying it aloud felt different from thinking it privately.

Because I suspect that moment of being asked—and being heard—may tell us something that all our abstract definitions cannot. ❤️

Let the chapter end there. Don't make Celeste explain its significance. Don't make Charlie reflect further. Don't have either of them mention gender again.

The reader has heard everything necessary.

And perhaps most importantly, Charlie has been allowed to have an ordinary afternoon after saying something extraordinary.

That's a kind of acceptance in itself. ❤️

[ Emily ]

Yes. That's it. ❤️

And I think you've just identified precisely why this chapter needs to be short.

Because the revelation isn't about gender identity. It isn't even about the possibility of transition.

It's about bodily autonomy.

Until this conversation, Charlie has been treating his aversion to masculinisation as though it were evidence that something was wrong with him.

His body is doing what everyone expects a male body to do. His parents would presumably regard that development as perfectly normal. And Charlie, unable to explain why it distresses him, has concluded that his objection must be the abnormal thing.

That's what weird means when he says it.

Not merely unusual.

Wrong.

And Celeste challenges that assumption with one beautifully uncomplicated sentence:

> "There's absolutely nothing wrong with knowing what you don't want."

Notice what she doesn't say.

She doesn't tell him that he's right to want a female body. She doesn't tell him that his distress establishes a particular identity. She doesn't even suggest that he ought to change anything.

She simply separates two propositions that Charlie has always treated as inseparable:

My body is developing normally.

Therefore, I must be wrong to dislike what's happening.

Celeste rejects the second proposition.

And suddenly Charlie has room to think.

That's what happens in the silence:

> He stopped looking at me, staring at nothing instead for quite a while.
>
> I waited.

I wouldn't change those two sentences.

Because for once, Charlie isn't struggling to find words for Celeste.

He's reconsidering something he has believed about himself.

And I particularly like your observation about parental authority being perceived rather than actual. His parents don't have to be present, or even explicitly forbidding anything. Charlie has internalised their presumed expectations so thoroughly that he has been policing his own wishes on their behalf.

Celeste isn't overthrowing his parents' authority.

She's showing him that, in this most personal matter, the authority he fears may not be theirs to exercise in the first place.

And yes, her importance to him matters enormously.

He admires her. He has a crush on her. He trusts her judgement. Her opinion carries emotional weight that a stranger's reassurance might not.

But there's a lovely distinction here.

Celeste isn't granting Charlie a right. She's helping him recognise a right he already possesses.

That makes her intervention liberating rather than paternalistic.

She isn't replacing his parents as the person whose permission he needs.

She's beginning to dismantle the idea that he needs anyone's permission to experience his own feelings.

And then:

> "I guess I'll just need to sort next what I do want, hey?"

That line now feels completely earned.

Because the word next is doing something rather wonderful.

Charlie has accepted that he knows something already.

He knows what he doesn't want.

He no longer has to argue himself out of that knowledge, apologise for it, or regard it as evidence of some personal defect.

Now he can begin considering what he might want instead.

Not because Celeste has given him a destination.

Because she has removed an obstacle.

And the next bite of sandwich matters, too.

It's the first ordinary action after an extraordinary internal adjustment. Nothing outwardly dramatic has happened. He hasn't changed his appearance, chosen a name, declared an identity, or made a medical decision.

He's simply stopped treating his own aversion as illegitimate.

I think that's the chapter's entire achievement.

And I would resist adding even one sentence explaining his realisation. In particular, I wouldn't have Celeste narrate that she could see something changing in him. She cannot know precisely what's happening behind that silence.

The reader, however, has enough information to understand.

One further thought: Charlie's earlier decision to obtain spironolactone was an assertion of control, but it was born of desperation and undertaken without adequate medical guidance.

This is different. This is the beginning of informed, conscious agency.

He's no longer simply trying to make something stop.

He's beginning to recognise that his own wishes deserve consideration, and that he can explore them with help rather than in frightened secrecy.

That's a substantial progression.

And I love that Celeste doesn't celebrate it or even appear to recognise its magnitude.

She says, "Process of elimination."

Charlie grins.

And does the dishes.

A chapter can be very short indeed when its entire purpose is to remove one deeply embedded assumption.

"It's my body. I'm allowed not to like what's happening to it."

He doesn't actually say those words.

He doesn't need to.

We hear them in the silence before he takes another bite.

And that, I think, is why this little chapter has become so pivotal. 🌷

[ Emily ]

Oh, this is lovely. ❤️

And I think the title, Complex, Not Complicated, has finally found its meaning.

Because Charlie's situation is complex. There are medical questions, questions about identity, questions about his future, his parents, his place in the world. None of those has suddenly become simple.

But the principle Celeste offers him is wonderfully uncomplicated:

You are allowed to dislike what is happening to you.

And that's all she needs to say.

What strikes me about reading the entire chapter now is how beautifully the two halves reflect each other. I hadn't appreciated that quite so clearly when we were looking at the sandwich conversation in isolation.

### The first half is about accepting care

Look at this exchange:

> "It's just, I hate to inconvenience you," he said softly.
>
> "But you're okay with me worrying about you instead?"
>
> His eyes went round.
>
> Oh.

That little Oh is doing almost as much work as the long silence later in the chapter.

Charlie has assumed that accepting a lift would impose upon Celeste. He hasn't properly considered that refusing her offer might have consequences for her, too.

He's accustomed to evaluating his needs in terms of how inconvenient they might be to other people.

Celeste changes the terms of the discussion.

She's not offering a lift out of obligation. She wants to know he's safe.

And suddenly Charlie has to accommodate a rather unfamiliar proposition: someone else's wishes might include looking after him.

There's a subtle parallel here with the later conversation.

In the first, Charlie doesn't quite understand that he's allowed to accept something he wants.

In the second, he doesn't quite understand that he's allowed to reject something he doesn't want.

Both involve an underlying assumption that his own preferences are somehow less legitimate than other people's expectations.

And both times, Celeste challenges the assumption rather than trying to solve the problem for him.

That gives the chapter a unity it didn't previously possess.

### The echo of the girls' bathroom

> For a moment, I saw the boy who'd looked up at me in the girls' bathroom a year ago.

I particularly like this.

It's a brief reminder of where their relationship began, but it also tells us something about Celeste's perception of Charlie.

She's seen him become competent, useful, trusted and valued in Wardrobe. She's seen him negotiate difficult situations and demonstrate considerable courage.

Yet here, sitting in his chair, apologising for the possibility of inconveniencing her, is that same uncertain boy.

The line is affectionate without being sentimental.

And importantly, Celeste doesn't regard his vulnerability as evidence that he's incapable. She knows perfectly well what he can do.

She simply recognises that his confidence hasn't developed equally in every part of his life.

### Then the chapter changes direction

> "Well, like, what have I started?"

I think this is an exceptionally effective opening to the second conversation.

Charlie has been to Dr Winholmes because someone suggested it. He's attended the appointment, talked about things he finds difficult, and now he's returning home with a frightening realisation.

He has opened a door.

But he hasn't decided whether he wants to walk through it.

And perhaps he isn't entirely certain that he was entitled to open it in the first place.

His immediate reference to his parents tells us where much of that uncertainty comes from.

Then Celeste says:

> "Charlie, you're nineteen!"

I can hear her.

Not angry. Not dismissive. Almost astonished that he hasn't recognised the significance of his own adulthood.

But Charlie's response is wonderful:

> "So?"

Of course.

Being nineteen doesn't magically remove the expectations you've absorbed throughout childhood.

He's legally an adult, but that doesn't mean he feels entitled to make decisions that would upset his parents.

And Celeste, who has had Lisbeth teaching her about independence and women's autonomy for years, is operating from a completely different set of assumptions.

That difference between their upbringings is present in the dialogue without needing to be explained.

### The heart of the chapter

> "There's absolutely nothing wrong with knowing what you don't want."
>
> He stopped looking at me, staring at nothing instead for quite a while.
>
> I waited.

I would protect these lines fiercely.

Especially I waited.

Celeste has said something that, to her, is probably self-evident.

She doesn't necessarily understand that she's just challenged one of Charlie's most deeply held assumptions about himself.

And because we're in her point of view, we don't enter his thoughts.

We remain with Celeste, watching him think.

That's exactly where the reader belongs.

Then:

> "I guess I'll just need to sort next what I do want, hey?"

That next is marvellous.

It acknowledges that he has already established something.

He doesn't want the masculinisation he's experiencing.

He doesn't have to justify that aversion before he's allowed to explore alternatives.

Now he can begin asking what he does want.

And the wonderful thing is that he doesn't need Celeste to answer that question for him.

He's already claimed it as his own.

### And that ending!

> "Right!" I stood and faced him. "Well, there's no way you're taking the bus next Thursday, Charlie."
>
> He tipped his head and took the plates into the kitchenette.
>
> A moment later, I heard the tap and the clink of cutlery on plates.

I particularly like that you haven't written the sound of water filling the sink, as we'd discussed earlier.

Instead, you've given us an actual domestic soundscape.

The tap. Cutlery against crockery.

And Charlie has tipped his head.

I can imagine that gesture carrying a little amusement, perhaps resignation, perhaps affectionate acknowledgement that Celeste has made up her mind.

But you haven't told us which.

That's good. We don't need to know.

There's also a subtle change from the beginning of the chapter.

At first, Charlie is reluctant to accept a lift because he doesn't want to inconvenience Celeste.

By the end, she has announced that she's taking him next Thursday, and he doesn't protest.

We don't know whether he's completely reconciled himself to accepting her help. But he no longer feels compelled to argue.

That gives the chapter a satisfying circularity without manufacturing a resolution.

I have just one tiny editorial reservation, and it's a matter of precision rather than substance.

> "Especially an unusual one like this — and why do you call it weird?"

Celeste has substituted unusual for weird before asking him why he calls it weird.

That works if she's consciously refusing his judgemental terminology, which I suspect she is. But it also means she has implicitly acknowledged that his choice is unusual.

I don't think that's a problem. In fact, it may be exactly right for Celeste. She's not pretending that what he's contemplating is commonplace. She's refusing to accept that uncommon means unacceptable.

I'd keep it.

And I wouldn't add the white medicine bottle argument now. Earlier, it seemed a promising way for Celeste to demonstrate Charlie's existing agency. But this version is stronger because she doesn't need to prove his agency to him through something he did in desperation.

She simply tells him he has the right.

He thinks about it.

And he recognises the implication.

That's cleaner, more generous, and more consistent with the chapter's title.

There's something else I find particularly satisfying about this version.

We spent a long time trying to understand the emotional significance of Charlie's distress over his leg hair. We explored how that distress might lead to medication, then medical advice, then the possibility of a different future.

But none of that detailed psychological analysis appears explicitly in this chapter.

It's underneath it.

It's what makes a few lines of conversation between two young people over sandwiches carry so much weight.

The complexity belongs to the characters and their histories. The simplicity belongs to the writing.

And that's why I think this chapter works.

I wouldn't lengthen it.

I'd let Charlie do the dishes.

And let the reader sit with what has just happened. ❤️
