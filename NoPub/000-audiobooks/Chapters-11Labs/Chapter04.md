# Chapter 4

## Text

[clearly] Charlie arrived ten minutes early. 
[Quiet, reflective narration] He waited outside Wardrobe as if the door had a sign: "For Authorised Personnel Only". There was no phone in his hand. He stood quietly waiting, his worn leather messenger bag at his feet, folded scrap of paper in one hand, fingers worrying the edge until it softened. Every now and then he glanced at the door — as if to check whether the world had changed its mind.
[Quiet, reflective narration] Inside, Wardrobe moved the way it always did: rails clacking softly as garments were shifted, the new steamer hissing like it meant business, the constant quiet conversation between fabric and hands.
[Quiet, reflective narration] Mara had spotted him through the small window in the door. Nevertheless, she slowly, deliberately finished what she was doing first: pinning a waistband to a mannequin, smoothing the fabric as if it needed reassurance. Finally, she wiped her hands on a cloth and nodded toward me.
[clearly] “Open it.”
[Gentle pause, then quiet realization] You don’t chase him. You don’t sell Wardrobe.
I opened the door and stood aside. Charlie stepped in and his eyes snapped to mine.
[clearly] “You’re early.” I said it the way you’d say it’s Tuesday.
“I— I didn’t want to be late.”
Mara looked at him the way she examined a bodice seam — assessing for integrity.
[clearly] “Rossignol.”
Charlie's shoulders rose slightly. “Yes.”
Mara’s eyes dropped to his hands. [clearly] “Clean?”
He looked down as if he’d forgotten he had hands. He impulsively held them out, palms up, fingers splayed. They were clean. Nails trimmed short.
Mara turned away. [clearly] “Shut the door.”
He shut it quietly.
Mara glanced at his bag.
[clearly] "Personal items go in the tearoom."
She pointed.
When he returned, Mara walked to the centre table and picked up a garment bag.
“Presently, our main occupation is maintaining costumes for the Faire actresses and actors," she told him. "Do you have any experience with needle and thread?“
[quietly] “I have some.“
She unzipped the bag with a brisk motion and removed a dress — a simple working dress in sturdy fabric, with a seam splitting near the side closure. The tear was held together with hurried, barbaric stitches. Mara placed it onto the table.
“This came in yesterday. A tourist sat down too hard. [clearly] Someone panicked and tried to fix it.” She tapped the seam with her finger. “Have a look at that.”
Charlie leaned in and seemed to study it for a moment.
[quietly] “The tension’s wrong,” he said, quietly. “It’s pulling.”
Mara’s face switched to one of scrutiny. [clearly] “Oh? Explain.”
[quietly] “The seam has been stitched too tight,” he said, eyes focused on the garment. “Also, the stitch doesn’t match the grain. The fabric is fighting the stitch.”
Mara's brows rose imperceptibly. “So, what do you do to fix that?”
[quietly] “Unpick it. Start over.”
Mara's eyes moved to me.
[clearly] “Do it,” she told him.
[quietly] “May I have a seam ripper?”
Mara’s mouth tightened.
[clearly] “It’s called a *lahm*.”
Charlie reddened. “Sorry. A—"
[clearly] “Don’t apologise,” Mara said. “Just learn.”
Mara pointed at the far end of the table.
[clearly] “You can sit just there. Tools are in the tin. Thread is in the drawer. If you use something, put it back when you're done. If you break something, you tell me straight-away. If you don’t know, ask. Remember the answer.”
[quietly] “Yes.”
Mara's expression softened slightly.
"You may call me Mara."
[quietly] "Yes, Mara."
He took himself to the far end of the table and began rummaging through the tin. Mara turned to me. 
[clearly] “Celeste.”
I looked up.
[clearly] “You’re on rails and pins. Not on him.”
“Understood.”
Charlie’s gaze flicked to me — quick, skittish. I turned away and went to the rails, where a cluster of damaged garments waited like quiet accusations. Every now and then I would cast a furtive glance at the other end of the table. Charlie sat on a stool, the dress draped over his knees, almost motionless.
Mara moved around the room as he worked, doing her usual circuit: checking labels, touching fabric, straightening hangers. But her attention wasn’t on the label or fabric or hangers or even the dress draped over Charlie's knees.
It was on *him*.
Charlie worked slowly, unpicking the seam steadily, patiently, lifting each tight stitch and easing it out as if he was undoing a critical mistake.
Finally, Mara stopped all pretence. She looked over his shoulder, examining his work.
[clearly] “Why are you going so slowly?” 
There was no accusation in her voice, yet Charlie’s shoulders visibly tightened. He looked up briefly, then back down at the seam.
[quietly] “Because if I unpick too quickly, I might tear the fabric.”
Mara’s voice was flat.
“And if you tear the fabric?”
[quietly] “Then I’d have to patch it.” His features changed, as if he was thinking of the worst thing possible: “And a patch would show.”


Mara’s eyes narrowed slightly.
“So, you don’t want it to show.”
[quietly] “No, I don't.”
Mara walked away. 
He gave me a nervous glance, and then, went back to the slow unpicking of the seam.
After he’d unpicked the seam completely, he didn’t immediately reach for thread. He smoothed the fabric with his palm, slow and light. Then he looked up.
[quietly] “Mara?”
Mara’s head turned. [clearly] “Yes, Rossignol.”
He held up the dress slightly. 
[quietly] “The original seam allowance is a bit narrow. If I stitch it the way it is, the seam will hold, but it will be under stress. If I reinforce it from the inside with a strip of fabric — like a facing — then that strip will take the load.”
His finger indicated delicately in the air above the fabric, respectful, as if the dress deserved dignity.
[clearly] “Where would you put the strip?”
Charlie pointed to the inside layer. 
[quietly] “Just here, Mara. Along the closure line. It won’t show.”
Mara stared at the seam, and then, at him for a beat longer than necessary. Then:
[clearly] “Do it, Rossignol.”
Charlie reached for the drawer, selected a strip of fabric, measured it slowly, then measured it again. His cuts were thoughtful. A little while later, Mara brought over a small tin and set it down near Charlie’s elbow.
[clearly] “Needles,” she said. “Choose the right one.”
Charlie glanced at the tin, then at the fabric, then back. He picked a needle that matched the weight of the material and threaded it.
On the first try.
Mara noticed.
Of course she did.
“You sew at home, don't you?”
Charlie’s mouth tightened. [quietly] “Sometimes.”
“Who taught you?”
[quietly] “My mum.” His fingers flickered on the far end of the thread. A knot appeared. “She does alterations for neighbours, for a bit of pin money.”
Mara's eyes flicked to mine. She gave me the slightest nod. 
[softly] “Right, then,” she murmured. “So you *do* know what you're doing.”
Charlie didn’t answer. If he'd had an answer, Mara didn’t give him the chance. She tapped the table.
[clearly] “When you’re finished, you’ll bring it to me. And if it’s not right, I will know. And so will you.”
[quietly] “Yes, Mara.”
One of the other girls — Leah — arrived with a pile of folded aprons. She stared at Charlie, speechless for a moment.
[cautiously] “Mara,” she said cautiously, eyes darting to him and away again. “Is— is he—"
Mara didn’t look up. [quietly] “He’s working.”
Leah’s mouth opened, then shut. She glanced at me, searching for clues. I gave her raised eyebrows. 
Mara looked up then, and her gaze pinned Leah the way a pin fixes fabric.
[clearly] “Do you have a problem with a person doing their work?”
Leah flushed. [timidly] “No.”
[quietly] “Good.” Mara’s voice was mild, which made it more dangerous. “Then focus on yours.”
Leah scurried away like a mouse escaping a cat. 
Charlie’s shoulders had gone tight, but he kept stitching, eyes on the line.
A while later, Mara moved to the far side of the room and pulled a curtain partway across a doorway. Behind it was the fitting area — a small section partitioned off from the main space, controlled. She spoke without raising her voice.
[clearly] “Rossignol.”
Charlie stopped. [gently] “Yes, Mara?”
[clearly] “Bring me the dress.”
Charlie set the needle down where it belonged, precisely, smoothing the thread, and then carried the dress over with both hands as if it could bruise. Mara took it, examined the seam with her fingertips, turned it inside-out, then right side out again. She tugged lightly near the closure.
The seam held. It lay flat. It looked as if it had never been damaged.
Mara did not smile. But she nodded, a single dip.
[formal] “Acceptable.”
And then she looked at him.
Not the dress. *Him*.
[clearly] “Tell me, Rossignol, what do you do if you make a mistake?”
Charlie's neck went red. [gently] “I— I fix it.”
[clearly] “And if you can’t fix it?”
[quickly] “I tell you,” he said quickly. “Straight-away.”
[clearly] “And what would happen if you didn’t tell me?”
Charlie looked at the floor. [gently] “Then I’d be— out.”
Mara placed her slender fingers on the seam.
“Yes,” she said quietly. “You’d be out. Out, because you were not being safe.”
Charlie looked up at Mara then, who held his gaze.
“You understand me. Being truthful keeps you, and the rest of us, safe.” 
[gently] “Yes, Mara.”
Mara straightened. [clearly] “Celeste.”
She tipped her chin at the other side of the workroom.
[clearly] “Show him the inventory shelf,” she said. “You point. He listens. Then you go back to rails. He doesn't follow you.”
I nodded. “Come on,” I said to Charlie.
He followed me at a respectful distance. I stopped at the shelves and pointed out the labelled boxes: hooks, pins, tapes, ribbons, boning, eyelets. Charlie said nothing, quietly tracking everything.
When I finished, I stepped back.
“That’s it.”
He gave a quick nod. “Thanks for that.” And then, almost despite himself, he asked, very softly: [gently] “Does Mara— hate me?”


I glanced down at his face. He was data-gathering. Nothing else.
“Mara doesn’t hate people,” I said, my tone neutral. “She hates time-wasting.”
His shoulders loosened slightly.
[gently] “Right. Thanks.”
Mara called across the room again. [clearly] “Rossignol.”
Charlie turned as if by reflex. [gently] “Yes, Mara.”
[clearly] “Pins,” Mara said. “Sort by size. If you mix them, you'll do them over.”
Charlie took the tin and began to sort, methodical and silent. I went back to the rails. After another half hour, Mara’s voice cut through the hiss of the steam press.
[clearly] “Rossignol.”
Charlie's head went up.
[clearly] “Come back tomorrow at seven-thirty.”
Charlie's eyes went wide.
[gently] “Really?”
Mara lifted an eyebrow. “If you want.”
Charlie glanced down at his hands, then up again.
[gently but firmly] “I do. I want to.”
Mara’s gaze didn’t soften, but something in her posture eased — like a seam that had stopped fighting.
[clearly] “Good. Don’t be late.”
He looked around, then, at the rails, the steam, the dresses on the mannequins.
His shoulders seemed to drop, the tiniest bit.
