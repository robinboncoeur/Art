Yes—**very reasonable**, and the zero/minimal-depth detail actually makes the scenario cleaner.

“Neo-labial abscess” is understandable, though in clinical dialogue they might more naturally call it a **small postoperative vulval abscess**, **labial abscess**, or **infected collection at the labial incision**. Zero-depth vulvoplasty still creates the external vulva—labia, clitoral complex and surrounding incision lines—so there is ample healing tissue in which a small fluid pocket, suture reaction, infected hair follicle or minor wound separation could produce a localised abscess. Infection remains a recognised complication even without a neovaginal canal. ([Transcare UCSF][1])

And yes: it could feel very much like your end-of-list “Bum-Puss” cases—**not an emergency catastrophe, but too painful and too anatomically sensitive to ignore**.

The likely presentation would be:

* increasing one-sided labial pain and swelling;
* a firm, exquisitely tender lump;
* local warmth and redness;
* perhaps scant purulent drainage from a healing incision;
* discomfort sitting, walking, or wearing underwear;
* possibly a low fever, but she need not be systemically unwell.

Because she is only six weeks postoperative, an ED clinician would be cautious. The question would not merely be, “Is this an ordinary boil?” but:

> Is this superficial, or connected to a deeper postoperative collection?

That justifies examination, blood tests, perhaps a wound swab, and often ultrasound if the depth is uncertain. They might also contact her surgical team—or the nearest plastics/urology/gynaecology service—because the anatomy is recently reconstructed.

## The most plausible outcome

If it is a discrete, superficial collection, the decisive treatment is usually **incision and drainage**, not antibiotics alone. Purulent skin abscesses are generally treated by opening and draining the pocket; antibiotics are added when there is surrounding cellulitis, fever, significant postoperative risk, immune compromise, or concern about deeper infection. ([Australian Prescriber][2])

So Charlotte’s ED course might be:

1. Examination confirms a small fluctuant swelling along one neo-labial incision.
2. Bedside ultrasound shows a shallow collection, perhaps two or three centimetres, without deep extension.
3. The registrar infiltrates local anaesthetic and opens it through the weakest point or existing wound edge.
4. A modest but deeply unpleasant quantity of pus drains.
5. It is irrigated; perhaps a small strip of packing is placed, perhaps not.
6. She receives oral antibiotics because it is postoperative and there is some surrounding cellulitis.
7. She goes home the same evening with next-day or 48-hour review.

That is probably more realistic than an IV drip unless she has fever, spreading redness, concerning bloods, or looks unwell. Queensland guidance distinguishes cellulitis associated with an abscess or wound and supports oral antistaphylococcal treatment for uncomplicated cases, while evidence of an abscess still points toward drainage. ([Queensland Health][3])

You could still justify **one IV dose** if the ED is initially worried, if the redness is spreading, or if they are waiting several hours for surgical review. But she does not need an overnight admission merely to make the episode legitimate.

In fact, same-day discharge may better preserve your desired scale:

> medically minor, emotionally enormous.

## The emotional sting

This version may be more intimate than the deeper-infection scenario because Charlotte is not protected by being gravely ill. She is fully conscious, alert, embarrassed, and expected to cooperate while several people inspect a painful reconstructed vulva.

She knows intellectually that this is a small abscess.

Emotionally, she sees:

* swelling where symmetry had only just begun to emerge;
* pus coming from the body she waited for;
* a clinician preparing to cut tissue that has already been cut and painstakingly rebuilt;
* the possibility, however irrational, that infection means contamination or failure.

And because it is “only a little abscess,” she may believe she has no right to be frightened.

That is where the scene becomes Charlotte.

She might apologise for making a fuss. The nurse might answer:

> “You’re six weeks out from major surgery. Coming in was exactly the right thing.”

She may ask whether the incision will spoil the result.

The registrar may say:

> “We’re opening the infected pocket, not undoing your surgery.”

Or, more clinically and therefore perhaps more powerful:

> “The reconstruction looks sound. This is local.”

That sentence could release everything she has been holding.

There is also no dilation problem now, which removes an unnecessary complication. Her postoperative care is external wound care: warm saline bathing or whatever the surgeon recommends, clean pads, antibiotics, analgesia, watching the redness, and follow-up. She may have to let Celeste inspect or dress the area at home—another deeply uncomfortable lesson in receiving care.

So yes: **a small postoperative labial abscess requiring ED assessment, bedside drainage and oral gorilla-cillins is entirely believable**. It sits exactly on your preferred border: painful, undignified and medically real, but never inflated into spectacle. 🌿

Sarah’s point should not be that Charlotte must stop trying to “earn” womanhood. That battle is over. Charlotte is a woman, full stop. The deeper correction is that she must stop treating **self-neglect as evidence of worth**.

Charlotte’s old logic has always been something like:

> If I notice everything, endure quietly, remain useful, and cause no inconvenience, I will be safe.

But now that she is loved, relied upon and woven into other women’s lives, that logic no longer harms only her. It frightens Celeste. It distresses Lauren. It forces Sarah into vigilance. It makes everyone else carry the consequences of Charlotte refusing to acknowledge her own limits.

So Sarah’s rebuke can be loving, but very firm:

> “You do not get to decide that your pain matters less because you can still stand up.”

Or:

> “Being useful does not mean making the rest of us discover you’ve been unwell for two days.”

And the key line you already have—

> “You don’t have to prove yourself, Charlotte. For any reason.”

—lands because it is not abstract reassurance. It is a verdict on a specific behaviour pattern.

Lauren’s distress from Scene 32 gives this scene continuity and consequence. Charlotte’s silence is no longer private modesty. It has emotional blast radius. That is the lesson she must begin to absorb.

The scene can therefore move from:

**embarrassment** → *this is disgusting and I have failed*
to **fear** → *something may be wrong with my body*
to **control** → *I can manage this if I stay composed*
to **rebuke** → *my composure is hurting people*
to **recognition** → *being cared for is part of belonging*
to **small surrender** → she admits the pain, accepts help, or stops apologising.

Not a grand transformation. Just one more brick removed from Charlie’s old survival architecture.

And that is enough. You have the medicine, the emotional stakes, and the thematic continuity. The scene should write itself only in the sense that it now knows what it is about—which is the best kind of readiness. 🌿
